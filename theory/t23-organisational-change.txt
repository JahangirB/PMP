title: Organisational change and adoption
summary: Why delivered is not the same as adopted, how people experience change, the main models for leading it, and how a project makes sure its result is used.
why: Most projects change the way people work. A new system, process or structure creates value only when people actually use it as intended, and people do not change their habits just because something new has been installed. Many projects declared successful at handover quietly fail afterwards, because the organisation never really changed. Leading the human side of change is how a project turns outputs into outcomes.

## Delivered is not adopted
Chapter 1 distinguished outputs, which a project delivers, from outcomes, which happen when the outputs are used. The gap between the two is **adoption**: the process by which people take up the new way of working and keep it up.

Adoption fails in familiar ways. Staff keep using the old spreadsheet alongside the new system. Managers continue to ask for the old reports. Workarounds appear for features that do not fit real work. Training is given once, months before go-live, and forgotten. Early problems confirm sceptics' doubts and the new way never recovers. Within a year, the expensive new capability is used for a fraction of what it was meant to do.

None of these failures is technical. All of them are predictable, and most are preventable if the project treats change management as part of its work from the start, rather than as a communication task at the end.

## How people experience change
Change affects people as individuals. Even a change they support asks them to give up familiar routines, learn new skills, and accept a period in which they are less competent than before. Change they did not choose may also threaten their status, relationships or job.

**William Bridges** distinguished **change**, the external event (the new system goes live on Monday), from **transition**, the internal psychological process people go through to come to terms with it. Transition has three phases. It begins with an **ending**: letting go of the old way and the identity that went with it. Then comes a **neutral zone**: an uncomfortable, confused in-between period when the old way has gone and the new one does not yet feel natural. Finally comes a **new beginning**: people commit to the new way and it becomes normal. Projects tend to focus on the change and neglect the transition, and in particular to underestimate how much people need help to let go of the old way.

Many people's experience of change follows a similar emotional curve, often drawn from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's work on grief or from Virginia Satir's change model: an initial dip in performance and morale after the change, followed by recovery and, if the change is good, eventual improvement beyond the starting point. The practical lesson is to expect the dip, plan support for it, and not judge the change by how things look in the first weeks.

## Models for leading change
Several well-known models offer structure for leading organisational change. They overlap more than they differ.

**Kurt Lewin** described change in three stages: **unfreeze** (create readiness by showing why the current state must change), **change** (move to the new state), and **refreeze** (stabilise the new state so it sticks). The model is simple, and its main point endures: change needs preparation before and reinforcement after, not just the move itself.

**John Kotter**, studying failed corporate transformations, proposed eight steps: create a sense of urgency; build a guiding coalition of influential people; form a strategic vision; communicate the vision widely; remove obstacles and empower people to act; generate short-term wins; sustain acceleration by building on those wins; and anchor the change in the culture. His analysis of why changes fail, such as declaring victory too soon or under-communicating the vision by a factor of ten, is as useful as the steps themselves.

The **ADKAR** model, developed by Jeff Hiatt at Prosci, focuses on the individual. For a person to change successfully, they need **awareness** of why the change is needed, the **desire** to take part, the **knowledge** of how to change, the **ability** to apply that knowledge in practice, and **reinforcement** to sustain it. The model is useful as a diagnostic: when someone is not adopting the change, which of the five is missing? Training addresses knowledge, but it cannot create desire in someone who does not see the point.

## Who adopts when
Everett Rogers's research on the **diffusion of innovations** found that people take up new ideas at different speeds, roughly in a bell curve: a small group of **innovators**, then **early adopters**, an **early majority**, a **late majority**, and finally **laggards**. Early adopters are often respected opinion leaders whose example persuades others; the late majority adopts once the change is the norm; laggards may adopt only when the old way is removed.

For a project, this suggests a practical approach: identify and work with the early adopters first, make their success visible, use them as champions and peer trainers, and plan explicit support for the late majority rather than expecting everyone to move at the speed of the enthusiasts.

## Making adoption part of the project
Change management works best when it is built into the project, not added at the end. Several practices help.

- **Assess the impact** early: who is affected, how much their work changes, and what they stand to gain or lose.
- **Involve affected people in design**, so the result fits real work and people feel ownership (Chapter 18).
- **Find and equip champions**, respected people in each affected group who understand the change and can support their colleagues.
- **Communicate the why, repeatedly**, in terms that matter to each group, and through people they trust.
- **Train close to the time of use**, in the context of real work, and follow up after go-live.
- **Plan for the dip**: extra support, floor walkers, quick fixes, and patience in the first weeks.
- **Remove the old way** when the new one is ready, so that people are not tempted back.
- **Align the system around the change**: reports, targets, procedures and incentives that still reward the old behaviour will undermine the new one.
- **Measure adoption**, not just delivery: who is using the new way, how, and with what results.

## Transition and handover
The project's last major responsibility for change is the **transition** of the result into operations. A good transition makes sure the operations team is ready to support the result, with the knowledge, documentation, tools and staff it needs; that users are trained and supported; that the old system or process is retired cleanly; and that the people who will own the benefits are in place and measuring them.

Transition often deserves its own plan, and sometimes its own phase. Projects that treat go-live as the finish line hand over results that the organisation is not ready to receive. Involving the operations team throughout the project, rather than at the end, is one of the most effective ways to make transition smooth.

## Organisational culture
Every change happens inside a culture: the shared assumptions, values and habits that shape how people behave, often without noticing. Edgar Schein described culture in layers: visible artefacts (offices, dress, rituals), espoused values (what the organisation says it believes), and underlying assumptions (what people actually take for granted). Changes that conflict with underlying assumptions meet resistance that no amount of communication about espoused values will overcome.

A project rarely has the power to change an organisation's culture, but it needs to understand it. An organisation where decisions are made by senior consensus will not adopt a system that assumes front-line staff make decisions alone. A project manager who reads the culture can design the change to work with it where possible, and can warn the sponsor where it must work against it.

> example: Northfield's first clinics adopted online booking quickly; the next ten did not. Amira's team looked at the difference. The first clinics had receptionist champions who had helped design the system, demonstrated it to patients and stopped offering phone booking for routine appointments once it was working. The later clinics had been trained by the project team, in a classroom, a month before go-live, and kept phone booking as the default. The team changed approach: each remaining clinic chose a champion who spent a week in a working clinic, and phone booking for routine appointments was gradually withdrawn once online use passed half. Adoption in the later clinics recovered within two months.

> myth: "Good systems sell themselves." Even excellent changes ask people to give up familiar habits and pass through a period of lower competence. Without deliberate support, many people will stay with what they know, however good the new way is.

> exam: Expect questions where a deliverable is accepted but not used, or where users resist a new process. The right answer usually addresses the human side: understand the resistance, involve users, provide training and support, find champions, communicate the benefits, and plan transition to operations. Questions also test that benefits are realised after handover and that the organisation's readiness for change is part of the project's concern.

## Key ideas
- Outputs create value only when adopted. Adoption fails for human reasons, which are predictable and mostly preventable.
- Bridges separates the external change from the internal transition: an ending, a neutral zone and a new beginning.
- Expect a dip in performance and morale after a change; plan support through it.
- Lewin, Kotter and ADKAR offer structure: prepare, move, reinforce; build urgency, coalitions and early wins; check awareness, desire, knowledge, ability and reinforcement.
- People adopt at different speeds; work with early adopters and champions, and support the late majority.
- Build change management into the project: involvement, champions, timely training, support, removal of the old way, and aligned incentives.

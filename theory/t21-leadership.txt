why: Project managers lead people they often do not manage, through change that many of those people did not ask for. Formal authority is limited, and the plan alone motivates nobody. What moves a project forward is the ability to set direction, earn trust and persuade, which is leadership. It can be learned, and the first step is understanding the options.

## What leadership is
Leadership has been defined in hundreds of ways, but most definitions share a core: leadership is the ability to influence a group towards achieving shared goals. It is distinct from position. A person can hold a senior title and fail to lead, and a team member with no title can lead a group through a crisis.

On projects, leadership shows in a few recurring tasks: establishing and communicating a clear vision of what the project is for; aligning people and groups who have different interests behind that vision; motivating and supporting the people doing the work; making and taking responsibility for difficult decisions; and modelling the behaviour the project needs, such as honesty about problems, respect for others and calm under pressure.

Chapter 3 noted the difference between management, which produces order and predictability, and leadership, which produces direction and change. Projects need both, and the same person usually has to provide both.

## Styles of leadership
Researchers have described many leadership styles. A handful are especially useful for project work.

- **Directive** or **autocratic** leaders decide and tell others what to do. This suits emergencies, inexperienced teams and situations needing fast, clear decisions, but stifles initiative if overused.
- **Participative** or **democratic** leaders involve the team in decisions. This builds commitment and draws on more knowledge, but takes time.
- **Laissez-faire** leaders largely leave the team to decide and act. This can suit highly capable, motivated teams, but becomes abdication if the team needs direction.
- **Transactional** leaders focus on exchanges: rewards for performance, correction for deviation, management by exception. This provides clarity but rarely inspires.
- **Transformational** leaders inspire people to exceed their own expectations through vision, personal example, intellectual stimulation and individual attention. This suits change and complex work.
- **Servant** leaders put the needs of the team first, removing obstacles and helping people grow, so that the team can perform (Chapter 3).
- **Charismatic** leaders inspire through personal energy and conviction, a powerful style that can also create dependence on one person.

No style is right everywhere. **Situational leadership**, developed by Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard, argues that leaders should adapt their style to the readiness of the people they lead. For someone new to a task and unsure of themselves, a directing style; for someone learning but still developing, coaching; for someone capable but hesitant, supporting; and for someone both capable and committed, delegating. The same project manager might direct a new graduate, coach a developing analyst and delegate to an experienced architect, all on the same day.

## Sources of power
Power, in this context, is the capacity to influence others. John French and Bertram Raven's classic study, later extended, identified several bases of power, and later writers have added more. A project manager can draw on several.

- **Legitimate** or **formal** power comes from position: the authority granted by the charter and the organisation. Project managers often have less of this than they need.
- **Reward** power is the ability to give things people value: recognition, interesting assignments, good references.
- **Coercive** or **punitive** power is the ability to impose negative consequences. It produces compliance at best, and resentment at worst; it should be a last resort.
- **Expert** power comes from knowledge and skill that others respect.
- **Referent** power comes from being liked and admired, so that others want to identify with you.
- **Informational** power comes from having or controlling information others need.
- **Relational** power comes from networks and relationships: knowing who to ask and having their goodwill.

Expert, referent, informational and relational power are personal: they belong to the person, not the position, and they cannot be granted by a charter. They are also the most durable, because they rest on others' willing respect. Project managers who build them, by being competent, well informed, connected, fair and reliable, can influence far beyond their formal authority.

## Influencing without authority
Much of a project manager's work involves getting people to do things they are not obliged to do: a functional manager to release a specialist, a director to make a decision quickly, a supplier to prioritise a fix, a team in another department to change its plans. A few principles help.

- **Understand what the other person wants.** Every stakeholder has goals, pressures and concerns. Influence starts with knowing them.
- **Find mutual benefit.** Allan Cohen and David Bradford's model of influence describes it as exchange: people help those who help them, in currencies they value, such as information, support, recognition or resources.
- **Build relationships before you need them.** Asking a stranger for a favour is hard; asking someone you have helped is easy.
- **Make it easy to agree.** A clear request, with the work already done to support it, gets a faster yes than a vague problem.
- **Use evidence and reason**, presented in the other person's terms.
- **Be consistent and trustworthy.** Reputations for honesty and fairness accumulate, and they make every future request easier.

Robert Cialdini's research on persuasion identified principles such as reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking and scarcity. They describe how people are actually influenced, and they are worth knowing so that you can use them honestly, and recognise when others are using them on you.

## Leading change and uncertainty
Projects lead people through change, and change makes people anxious. Leadership in these conditions involves explaining why the change matters, being honest about what is known and unknown, giving people a sense of control where possible, acknowledging losses, and keeping attention on the purpose. Leaders who pretend to certainty they do not have lose credibility when events contradict them; leaders who share their uncertainty while showing how the project will navigate it tend to keep their teams' trust.

## The leader's own development
Leadership begins with self-awareness: understanding your own strengths, preferences, blind spots and effect on others. Feedback from colleagues, reflection on experience, coaching and mentoring all build it. Many leadership failures on projects are failures of self-awareness: a project manager who does not realise that their impatience silences the team, or that their wish to be liked stops them confronting a problem.

**Emotional intelligence** matters here as well as in teams (Chapter 20): managing one's own reactions under pressure, empathising with others, and handling difficult conversations constructively. So does **integrity**, consistently acting on stated values, because people follow leaders they trust.

> example: Halfway through Northfield's rollout, the trust announced a restructure that put several clinic administrators' jobs at risk. Morale in the clinics collapsed, and some staff saw the booking system as the cause. Amira could not change the restructure, and she did not pretend otherwise. She met the clinic managers, acknowledged the uncertainty, explained truthfully what the system would and would not change about their roles, and arranged for HR to answer questions she could not. She also slowed the rollout at two clinics where staff were most affected. The project lost three weeks and kept the clinics' cooperation, which the sponsor agreed was the better trade.

> myth: "Leaders are born, not made." Some traits help, but studies of leadership development consistently find that the behaviours of effective leaders can be learned: setting direction, listening, giving feedback, adapting style, building trust. Most good project leaders became good through experience and deliberate reflection.

> exam: The exam strongly favours servant leadership and collaborative, participative styles, with directive behaviour reserved for emergencies or inexperienced teams. Expect questions on situational leadership, the types of power (and the preference for expert and referent over coercive), and emotional intelligence. In scenarios, the right answer usually influences through understanding, evidence and relationships rather than formal authority or threats, and adapts the leadership approach to the people and the situation.

## Key ideas
- Leadership is influencing a group towards shared goals; it is distinct from position.
- Styles range from directive to delegating; situational leadership matches style to the people's readiness.
- Power comes from position, reward, coercion, expertise, admiration, information and relationships. The personal sources are the most durable.
- Influence without authority rests on understanding others' goals, mutual benefit, relationships, clear requests, evidence and trust.
- Leading change means honesty about uncertainty and loss, and a steady focus on purpose.
- Leadership is learned, starting with self-awareness, emotional intelligence and integrity.

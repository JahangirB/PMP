title: Stakeholders and engagement
summary: Who stakeholders are, how to understand their interests and influence, how to engage rather than merely inform them, and how to work with resistance.
why: Projects are judged by people, and the people who judge them are rarely only the sponsor. Users, managers, regulators, suppliers, unions, neighbours and the public all have a stake, and their support or opposition can decide the outcome. Many projects that fail technically succeed in the end because stakeholders stayed with them; many technically excellent projects fail because the people they affected were never brought along.

## Who stakeholders are
A **stakeholder** is any individual, group or organisation that may affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by a project. The last clause matters: people who believe a project affects them are stakeholders, whether or not they are right, because their beliefs shape their behaviour.

Stakeholders can be grouped in several ways. **Internal** stakeholders work in the organisation: the sponsor, the team, functional managers, the project management office, other projects competing for the same people. **External** stakeholders do not: customers, users, suppliers, regulators, partners, communities, the media. Some are **supporters**, some **opponents**, many **neutral** or **unaware**. Some are individuals, others groups who may not speak with one voice.

Two groups are frequently forgotten. The first is the people who will **operate and support** the result: the help desk, the maintenance team, the clinic receptionists. They inherit the project's decisions and often know exactly what will go wrong. The second is people who lose something because of the project: their role, their influence, their familiar way of working. They are often the most important stakeholders to understand, precisely because their interests run against the project's.

## Identifying stakeholders
Identification starts early (Chapter 9) and continues throughout, because new stakeholders appear as the project develops. Useful sources include the charter and business case, organisation charts, the people who will use or support the result, regulations, contracts, and interviews with experienced people who know the organisation. A simple question asked of every stakeholder found, "who else should we be talking to?", reliably uncovers more.

The result is usually recorded in a **stakeholder register**: who each stakeholder is, their role, their interests and expectations, their influence, their current attitude, and how the project intends to engage them. On sensitive projects, parts of the register may be confidential, because candid notes about influence and resistance are not always comfortable reading for the people described.

## Understanding stakeholders
Stakeholders differ in how much they can affect the project and how much they care. Several simple models help make sense of this.

The most common is the **power-interest grid**, which places each stakeholder by their power over the project and their interest in it. Those with high power and high interest need to be **managed closely**: engaged actively and involved in decisions. Those with high power and low interest should be **kept satisfied**: consulted and kept comfortable, without overwhelming them with detail. Those with low power and high interest should be **kept informed**: they care, and they can often help or provide early warning. Those with low power and low interest need **monitoring**, with minimal effort.

[figure: t18-grid]

Variations use influence instead of power, or impact instead of interest. The **salience model** adds a third dimension: **power** (the ability to impose their will), **legitimacy** (the appropriateness of their involvement) and **urgency** (the need for immediate attention). Stakeholders who have all three, called definitive stakeholders, demand the most attention.

These models are tools for thinking, not for labelling people permanently. Stakeholders move: a low-interest manager becomes highly interested when the project proposes to take two of her staff. Revisit the analysis regularly.

## Engagement, not just communication
There is an important difference between **informing** stakeholders and **engaging** them. Informing tells people what is happening. Engaging involves them: seeking their views, understanding their concerns, giving them a genuine say in decisions that affect them, and building relationships of trust.

Engagement matters for three reasons. Stakeholders hold knowledge the project needs. People who have shaped a change are far more likely to support it than people who have had it imposed on them. And relationships built early provide goodwill that the project can draw on when things go wrong, as they will.

A useful tool is the **stakeholder engagement assessment matrix**, which records for each stakeholder their **current** level of engagement and the **desired** level. Levels are often described as:

- **Unaware** of the project and its potential effects.
- **Resistant**: aware, and resistant to the change.
- **Neutral**: aware, neither supportive nor resistant.
- **Supportive**: aware and supportive.
- **Leading**: aware and actively engaged in making the project succeed.

The gap between current and desired engagement for each important stakeholder shows where effort should go. Not every stakeholder needs to be leading; the aim is the level of engagement the project actually needs from each one.

## Planning and doing engagement
Engagement planning decides how to move each important stakeholder from their current to their desired level: what they need to know, what they need to be involved in, who will build the relationship, how often, and through which channels. The plan may be a formal document or a few lines in the stakeholder register.

In practice, engagement happens through many small actions: a briefing before a steering committee so a director is not surprised; a workshop where users shape a design; a visit to a site to hear concerns first hand; a demonstration of a working prototype; a quick call to explain a delay before it appears in a report. The project manager does not have to do all of it personally, but should make sure it is happening and that the people doing it understand what the project needs from each relationship.

Expectation management is part of engagement. Many conflicts arise because stakeholders expected something the project never intended to deliver. Making expectations explicit, about scope, timing, involvement and what the result will and will not do, and revisiting them as the project changes, prevents disappointment from turning into opposition.

## Working with resistance
Resistance is a normal response to change, and it is often rational. People resist because they fear losing status, skills, control or their jobs; because they have seen similar initiatives fail; because the change creates work for them without benefits; because they were not consulted; or because they know something the project has missed.

Treating resistance as an obstacle to be overcome tends to harden it. Treating it as information tends to soften it. Useful responses include:

- **Listen first.** Find out what exactly people object to and why. Often the objection is narrower and more reasonable than it first appears.
- **Look for the legitimate concern.** If people say the new system will slow them down, test whether it will. If they are right, fix it.
- **Involve the resisters.** People invited to help solve a problem often become its strongest advocates.
- **Be honest about losses.** If some people will lose something, say so and explain why the change is still worth it, rather than pretending everyone wins.
- **Use the right messenger.** A respected peer is often more persuasive than the project team.
- **Escalate when necessary.** Some resistance reflects genuine conflicts of interest that only senior management can resolve.

> example: Several senior receptionists at Northfield opposed online booking. Amira spent an afternoon at the busiest clinic and discovered their concern was specific and sensible: patients who booked online often chose the wrong appointment type, and receptionists spent time fixing the mistakes. She brought two receptionists into the design team. Their suggestions, clearer appointment descriptions and a warning when patients chose specialist slots, cut booking errors sharply. One of them later trained receptionists at the other clinics.

## Stakeholders in adaptive projects
Adaptive approaches build stakeholder engagement into their rhythm. The **product owner** represents the customer and business stakeholders day to day. **Sprint reviews** invite stakeholders to see working results regularly and influence what comes next. Frequent delivery means stakeholders form opinions based on the real product rather than documents. And the transparency of backlogs and boards lets interested stakeholders see what is happening without asking.

The risk in adaptive projects is that the product owner becomes the only channel, and stakeholders the product owner does not represent, such as operations, security, finance or regulators, are engaged too late. Good adaptive teams invite these stakeholders to reviews, include their needs in the backlog, and make sure the product owner has the relationships and authority to represent the wider organisation.

## Ethics and stakeholders
Stakeholder management can slide into manipulation: telling different groups different stories, hiding inconvenient facts, or treating people as obstacles to be managed rather than as people with legitimate interests. Beyond being wrong, this tends to fail, because stakeholders talk to each other and trust, once lost, is very hard to regain. The more durable approach is honesty, consistency and respect, including for those who oppose the project. Chapter 24 returns to ethics.

> myth: "Stakeholder management means keeping stakeholders happy." It means engaging them appropriately and honestly. Sometimes that involves telling them things they do not want to hear, or making decisions they oppose. The aim is informed support or, at least, informed acceptance, not universal satisfaction.

> exam: The exam has many stakeholder scenarios. Common themes: identify stakeholders early and continually; analyse them (power and interest, salience) and record them in the register; engage rather than only inform; when a stakeholder resists, first understand their concern, usually by meeting them; when a new stakeholder appears late, add them to the register and assess their needs rather than ignoring them; use the engagement assessment matrix to compare current and desired engagement. Answers that bypass, ignore or merely inform a powerful stakeholder are usually wrong.

## Key ideas
- Stakeholders include anyone who affects, is affected by, or believes they are affected by the project, especially those who will support the result or lose something.
- Identification is continual; the stakeholder register records who they are and how to engage them.
- Power-interest grids and the salience model help decide where to focus; stakeholders move, so revisit them.
- Engaging means involving people in decisions, not just informing them; the engagement matrix compares current with desired engagement.
- Resistance is often rational and informative; listen, find the legitimate concern, involve resisters, and be honest about losses.
- Adaptive projects engage stakeholders through reviews and the product owner, but must not forget stakeholders the product owner does not represent.

title: Closing, learning and getting better
summary: How to close a project properly, why most lessons are identified and never learned, how teams and organisations actually learn, and how a project manager keeps growing.
why: Projects end, but the organisation, the people and the profession continue. A well-closed project leaves its result in safe hands, its people recognised and ready for what comes next, its contracts settled and its knowledge available to others. A poorly closed one leaves loose ends that cost money for years and lessons that every later project relearns at its own expense.

## Why closing matters
Closing is the most neglected part of most projects. By the time the main deliverables are accepted, the team is tired, people are being pulled onto new work, the sponsor's attention has moved on, and the remaining tasks seem administrative. Yet several things can only be done at the end, and if they are skipped, the costs appear later in other budgets.

- Deliverables that were never formally accepted become disputes.
- Contracts left open accumulate charges and claims.
- Operations teams inherit results they do not fully understand.
- Team members leave without recognition or a clear next role.
- Knowledge disperses with the people who held it.
- Benefits nobody owns are never measured or realised.

Closing well is a final act of stewardship (Chapter 5).

## Closing a project or phase
Closing applies to each phase as well as to the whole project, and to projects that end early as well as those that finish. The main activities are similar in each case.

- **Confirm acceptance.** Obtain formal acceptance of the final deliverables from the sponsor or customer, against the agreed acceptance criteria.
- **Complete the transition** to operations (Chapter 23): support arrangements, documentation, training and handover of responsibility.
- **Close contracts**: confirm all deliverables have been received and accepted, settle final payments and outstanding claims, and record supplier performance (Chapter 15).
- **Settle finances**: final costs, release of unused reserves, closure of cost accounts.
- **Release resources**: people, equipment and facilities, with appropriate notice and support for team members moving to new work.
- **Archive records**: plans, decisions, reports, contracts and correspondence, in a form others can find.
- **Capture and share lessons learned.**
- **Hand over benefits**: make sure the benefits owners are in place, the measures are defined, and a benefits review is scheduled (Chapter 4).
- **Report**: a final report summarising what was achieved against the objectives, the final cost and schedule, the benefits expected and how they will be tracked, and the main lessons.
- **Celebrate and recognise** the team's work.

The last point is not frivolous. Recognising people's contribution, publicly and specifically, matters for their motivation, their careers and their willingness to work on the next project. It also marks the end, which helps people move on.

## Projects that end early
Not every project reaches its planned end. Some are cancelled because the need has disappeared, the business case has failed, priorities have changed or the project cannot succeed. Early termination can be the right decision, and good portfolio management treats it as such (Chapter 4).

A terminated project still needs closing: work completed so far should be documented and, where useful, preserved; contracts terminated on the terms they allow; people released with care; and, above all, the reasons understood and recorded. Projects stopped without analysis tend to be followed by similar projects that fail in similar ways.

## Lessons identified, lessons learned
Almost every organisation collects lessons learned. Far fewer actually learn from them. The common pattern is that a lessons-learned workshop is held at the end of the project, a report is written and stored, and nobody reads it again. The lessons have been **identified** but not **learned**, because nothing changed as a result.

Several things make the difference.

- **Capture lessons throughout the project**, not just at the end, when memories have faded and the people who knew have left. A lessons learned register updated at every phase and retrospective holds far more than an end-of-project workshop.
- **Make lessons specific and actionable.** "Communication could have been better" teaches nothing. "Clinic managers were not told about go-live dates until two weeks before, so they could not plan staff training; next time, confirm dates with them at least six weeks ahead" teaches something.
- **Find the cause.** A lesson that describes what went wrong without why it happened will not prevent it next time.
- **Include what went well.** Successful practices are lessons too, and are easier to repeat if recorded.
- **Feed lessons into something that changes behaviour**: templates, checklists, methods, training, estimating data or governance requirements. A lesson that changes a standard process is learned by every future project automatically.
- **Look at lessons before starting**, not just after finishing. The most effective use of lessons learned is at the start of the next similar project.

## Retrospectives
Adaptive teams hold **retrospectives** at the end of every iteration: a short, regular reflection on how the team is working, leading to one or two concrete improvements to try in the next iteration. Retrospectives turn learning from an end-of-project event into a habit, and because each improvement is tried immediately, the team quickly learns whether it worked.

Good retrospectives share a few features. They are safe, so people can be honest; Norm Kerth's prime directive, which asks participants to assume everyone did the best job they could with what they knew at the time, helps. They focus on the system rather than blaming individuals. They vary their format to avoid going stale. And above all, they lead to action: a retrospective that produces observations but no changes is a discussion, not an improvement.

Predictive projects can borrow the practice. A short retrospective at the end of each phase, or each month, captures lessons while they are fresh and lets the project itself benefit from them.

## How organisations learn
Chris Argyris and Donald Schön distinguished two kinds of learning. **Single-loop learning** corrects actions within existing assumptions: the estimate was too low, so next time add more contingency. **Double-loop learning** questions the assumptions themselves: why are our estimates consistently low, and what about how we estimate, approve or report needs to change? Single-loop learning fixes problems; double-loop learning prevents whole classes of them.

Organisations that learn well from projects tend to share some features: leaders who welcome bad news, time set aside for reflection, people who move between projects carrying knowledge, communities of practice where practitioners share experience, project management offices that act as stewards of learning rather than enforcers of templates, and processes that are actually changed in response to lessons.

## Growing as a project manager
The same principles apply to individuals. Project managers grow by reflecting on their own experience, honestly and regularly; by seeking feedback from sponsors, team members and stakeholders; by learning from others through mentoring, reading, communities and courses; and by deliberately taking on work that stretches them.

Certifications such as the PMP formalise part of this. They establish a common foundation of knowledge, and their renewal requirements encourage continued learning. But a certification is a starting point. The practitioners who become excellent are those who keep asking, after every project, what they would do differently, and who then actually do it differently next time.

> example: Northfield's project closed fourteen months after it began. Amira's final report showed online bookings at forty-six per cent of routine appointments in clinics live for more than six months, against a target of forty per cent; phone waits down to four minutes; missed appointments unchanged; and final cost six per cent over budget, with the causes explained. The lessons register had sixty entries, from which the trust's project office changed three things: its estimating guidance for integration work, its standard training approach (champions rather than classrooms) and its template for business cases, which now required a named benefits owner. The benefits review a year later found the online share had reached fifty-eight per cent. The receptionist who had first opposed the system was running training for the next hospital.

> myth: "Lessons learned is an end-of-project activity." By the end, most of the people who knew have moved on and much of what happened has been forgotten. Learning needs to happen throughout, and it only counts when it changes what happens next.

> exam: Expect questions on what closing involves: formal acceptance, transition, closing procurements, releasing resources, archiving, final reports and lessons learned. Typical scenarios test that you obtain formal acceptance before closing, close contracts properly, update the lessons learned register throughout the project rather than only at the end, and close even a terminated project in an orderly way. In adaptive scenarios, retrospectives are the team's continuous learning mechanism.

## Key ideas
- Closing is stewardship: acceptance, transition, contracts, finances, resources, records, lessons, benefits handover, reporting and recognition.
- Terminated projects need closing too, with their reasons understood and recorded.
- Lessons are learned only when something changes: capture them throughout, make them specific, find causes, and feed them into processes and the start of future projects.
- Retrospectives make learning a regular habit; they must lead to action.
- Single-loop learning fixes problems; double-loop learning questions the assumptions behind them.
- Project managers grow through reflection, feedback, learning from others and deliberate stretch.

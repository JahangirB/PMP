summary: How projects connect to strategy: portfolios that choose the work, programmes that coordinate it for benefits, and the governance and structures around them.
why: No project exists on its own. It competes with other work for money and people, depends on decisions made above it, and contributes, or fails to contribute, to what the organisation is trying to achieve. A project manager who understands the system around the project can work with it instead of being surprised by it.

## From strategy to delivery
An organisation's strategy says where it wants to go. Its leaders cannot deliver a strategy directly; they have to turn it into specific pieces of work, choose among the many pieces they could do, fund some and decline others, and then make sure the chosen work actually produces the intended change. Three levels of management do most of this: portfolio, programme and project management. Operations, which runs the organisation day to day, both receives what the projects deliver and supplies many of the people who do the work.

[figure: t4-hierarchy]

The levels answer different questions. Portfolio management asks: are we doing the right work? Programme management asks: are these related pieces of work together producing the benefits we need? Project management asks: are we doing this piece of work right? All three are needed. An organisation can execute its projects flawlessly and still fail if it chose the wrong ones, or if the right projects never add up to the change it intended.

## Portfolios: choosing the right work
A **portfolio** is a collection of projects, programmes and sometimes operational work, managed together to achieve strategic objectives. The items in a portfolio need not be related to each other; what they share is a pool of money, people and attention, and a strategy they are meant to serve.

Portfolio management is mostly about choice. There are always more good ideas than resources to pursue them, so someone must decide which to start, which to continue, which to pause and which to stop. Typical activities include:

- **Identifying and categorising** candidate work: growth initiatives, efficiency improvements, mandatory regulatory changes, maintenance of existing assets.
- **Evaluating and selecting** candidates against criteria such as strategic fit, expected benefit, cost, risk and urgency.
- **Balancing** the portfolio, so that it is not all high-risk innovation or all low-risk maintenance, and so that the organisation is not depending on too many uncertain bets at once.
- **Allocating capacity**, because the binding constraint is often not money but the handful of people with scarce skills.
- **Reviewing** the portfolio regularly and reallocating as strategy, performance and circumstances change.

The hardest part of portfolio management is stopping things. Projects acquire momentum and champions, and money already spent feels like a reason to continue. Economists call this the sunk cost fallacy: past spending cannot be recovered whatever you do next, so it should not influence the decision about whether to continue. The right question is always whether the remaining cost is worth the remaining benefit. A healthy portfolio cancels projects that have stopped being worth finishing, and treats that as good management rather than failure.

## How organisations compare candidate projects
Portfolio decisions often rest on a business case for each candidate (Chapter 9 looks at business cases in detail). Several financial measures appear again and again.

- **Payback period**: how long until the money spent is recovered from the returns. Simple and intuitive, but it ignores everything that happens after payback and the time value of money.
- **Net present value**: the sum of all future cash flows, each discounted back to today's money at a chosen rate, minus the investment. A positive value means the project earns more than the discount rate. It is the measure finance specialists generally trust most for comparing investments.
- **Internal rate of return**: the discount rate at which the net present value would be zero. Higher is better, and it can be compared with the organisation's cost of capital.
- **Benefit-cost ratio**: the present value of benefits divided by the present value of costs. Above one, benefits exceed costs.

Financial measures are only part of the picture. Many valuable projects have benefits that are hard to price: compliance with the law, safety, reputation, staff wellbeing, building a capability for the future. Many organisations therefore also use **scoring models** that rate each candidate against weighted criteria, financial and non-financial, so that different kinds of value can be compared on one scale. The weights themselves are a statement of strategy, and arguing about them is often more useful than arguing about individual projects.

> example: Northfield Health Trust's portfolio board had more than forty candidate projects competing for one year's capital budget. The online booking project scored well on strategic fit and patient experience and had a reasonable financial case, mainly from reduced call-centre staffing. A new cancer treatment wing scored higher on patient outcomes but needed three years of funding. A mandatory upgrade of the patient records system scored low on everything except compliance, which the board treated as a gate rather than a score: it had to be done regardless. The board's decisions were shaped as much by which projects needed the same scarce clinical informatics staff as by the scores themselves.

## Programmes: coordinating for benefits
A **programme** is a group of related projects, and sometimes ongoing activities, managed in a coordinated way to obtain benefits that would not be available from managing them separately. The key word is benefits. A programme exists because the value lies in the combination.

Suppose Northfield wanted to transform outpatient care. That would involve online booking, a new clinic scheduling system, changes to how referrals arrive from family doctors, new reminders by text message, a redesign of the clinic reception process, and training for hundreds of staff. Each could be a project. But booking without scheduling is of limited use, reminders without accurate bookings irritate patients, and none of the technology matters unless staff change how they work. Managing these as a programme allows someone to sequence them sensibly, manage the dependencies between them, share resources, and keep the whole effort aimed at the benefits: fewer missed appointments, shorter waits, lower costs.

Programme management therefore differs from project management in emphasis. Projects focus on delivering outputs within constraints. Programmes focus on achieving outcomes and benefits over a longer period, often while the organisation's needs are evolving. Programme managers spend much of their time on benefits management, governance, stakeholder engagement across many groups and the interfaces between projects. They are expected to adjust the set of projects as they learn: starting new ones, reshaping some, closing others early.

Not every collection of projects is a programme. If projects merely share a sponsor or a department, but their benefits do not depend on one another, they belong in a portfolio, not a programme.

## Benefits management
Because benefits usually appear after a project hands over, they are easy to lose. Benefits management is the discipline of keeping them in view across the whole life of an investment.

It typically involves identifying the intended benefits and how they will be measured; assigning each benefit an **owner**, usually a manager in the business who will be around after the project closes; planning how and when each benefit will be realised; tracking the measures over time; and doing what is needed to sustain the benefits once they arrive. The tools vary, but the core artefact is often a benefits register or benefits realisation plan, linked to the business case.

The project manager's part in this is to deliver outputs that make the benefits achievable, to flag early when design choices or scope changes threaten them, and to plan the transition so that the business is ready to use what is delivered. The benefits owner's part is to make the change stick in operations and to report honestly on whether the benefits came.

## Governance: who decides what
**Governance** is the framework of rules, roles, decision rights and processes by which an organisation directs and controls its work. Project governance answers questions such as: Who can authorise a project? Who can approve a change to its scope or budget? When must decisions be escalated, and to whom? How is performance reported, and to whom? When is a project reviewed, and on what basis can it be stopped?

Good governance is proportionate. A small, low-risk project needs a sponsor and a simple approval for changes. A large, high-risk programme may need a steering committee, stage gates, independent assurance reviews and formal reporting to the board. Too little governance lets projects drift and hide problems; too much slows decisions, buries teams in reporting and encourages people to manage the governance instead of the work.

Several roles appear in most governance arrangements.

- **The sponsor** is the senior person who champions the project, provides or secures the funding, owns the business case and makes or obtains the decisions above the project manager's authority. A sponsor who is engaged, available and willing to make hard calls is one of the strongest predictors of success.
- **A steering committee or project board** brings together senior representatives of the business, the users and the suppliers to give direction and approve major decisions.
- **Stage or phase gates** are decision points at the end of a phase where governance reviews progress and the business case and decides whether to continue, change course or stop.
- **The project management office**, or PMO, provides support, standards and oversight.

## The project management office
PMOs vary so much that the name alone tells you little. They are often described by how much control they exercise.

- A **supportive** PMO provides templates, training, good practice and lessons learned. Projects can take or leave its help. Its control is low.
- A **controlling** PMO requires projects to follow its methods and use its templates, and checks compliance through reviews. Its control is moderate.
- A **directive** PMO manages the projects directly, assigning its own project managers to them. Its control is high.

PMOs may also manage the portfolio, maintain shared resources, run assurance reviews or coach teams in adaptive practices. Some organisations now describe theirs as a value management office or an agile centre of excellence, reflecting a shift from enforcing compliance to helping the organisation choose and deliver valuable work. A PMO is most useful when the people in projects see it as making their work easier; when it is seen mainly as a source of forms, its standards tend to be followed on paper and ignored in practice.

## Organisational structures
How an organisation is structured shapes how much authority a project manager has and where the team's loyalties lie.

In a **functional** organisation, staff are grouped by specialism (engineering, finance, marketing) under functional managers. Projects are run within or across departments, often by a coordinator with little authority. Specialist expertise is strong and careers are clear, but cross-department projects are slow, and the project usually loses when it competes with departmental priorities.

In a **projectised** organisation, staff are grouped around projects, and the project manager has substantial authority over the budget and the team, who often work on the project full-time. Construction firms and consultancies often work this way. Focus is strong, but specialists can become isolated from their professional peers, and people may have no obvious home when a project ends.

A **matrix** organisation combines the two: staff belong to functional departments but are assigned to projects, and answer to both a functional manager and a project manager. In a weak matrix, the functional manager holds most of the power and the project manager is closer to a coordinator. In a strong matrix, the project manager holds more authority and may be full-time. A balanced matrix sits between. Matrices make efficient use of specialists, but they create two bosses, and with them conflicts over priorities that the project manager must negotiate.

[figure: t4-structures]

Many organisations today are hybrids, and some organise around long-lived, cross-functional product teams rather than temporary projects. The question to ask in any organisation is practical: who controls the people, who controls the money, and who decides priorities when they conflict? The answers tell a project manager where they will need to build influence.

## Organisational context: what the project inherits
Every project inherits a context it did not choose. Standards traditionally divide it into two kinds of factor.

**Enterprise environmental factors** are conditions outside the project team's control that affect the project. Some are internal: the organisation's culture, structure, governance, infrastructure, available skills and tolerance for risk. Some are external: market conditions, laws and regulations, industry standards, economic conditions, and the physical environment.

**Organisational process assets** are the plans, processes, policies, procedures and knowledge bases the organisation already has and the project can use: templates, methods, past project files, lessons learned, estimating data, and approved supplier lists.

The distinction is useful mainly as a prompt. Before planning anything, a project manager should find out what the organisation already knows and already requires, and what in the environment will constrain the project. Reinventing an estimating method that the organisation has refined over twenty projects, or discovering halfway through that a procurement policy forbids the supplier you chose, are both avoidable.

> myth: "Governance is bureaucracy that slows projects down." Badly designed governance does. Good governance speeds projects up, because decisions get made by the right people at the right time, and teams do not waste effort on work that would have been stopped anyway.

> exam: Expect situational questions in which the project manager hits the limit of their authority: a change that needs a budget increase, a conflict between two functional managers over a shared expert, a sponsor who has stopped attending meetings. The right answer usually respects the governance framework: analyse the impact, then take it to the person or body with the authority to decide, rather than deciding alone or ignoring it. Questions about benefits often hinge on whether you remember that benefits are owned by the business and realised after handover.

## Key ideas
- Portfolios choose the right work for the strategy; programmes coordinate related work for combined benefits; projects deliver outputs.
- Stopping projects that are no longer worth finishing is a sign of good portfolio management. Sunk cost should not drive the decision.
- Financial measures (payback, net present value, internal rate of return, benefit-cost ratio) are useful but partial; scoring models make non-financial value comparable.
- Benefits are owned by the business and usually appear after handover, so they need owners, measures and tracking.
- Governance defines decision rights. It should be proportionate to the size and risk of the work.
- Structure decides authority: functional, matrix (weak, balanced, strong) and projectised organisations give project managers very different levels of control.
- Every project inherits environmental factors that constrain it and process assets it can use.

summary: Deciding whether to make or buy, the main contract types and how they share risk, incentive contracts and the point of total assumption, selecting suppliers, and running contracts well.
why: Much project work is done by other organisations: contractors, suppliers, consultants, vendors. A contract is a set of promises backed by law, and it shapes the behaviour of both sides for the life of the work. Choosing the wrong kind of contract can make an honest supplier act against your interests; choosing the right one aligns everyone's incentives with the project's success.

## Make or buy
Every piece of work can, in principle, be done inside the organisation or bought from outside. The **make-or-buy decision** weighs the options. Reasons to buy include lacking the skills or capacity, needing specialised equipment, wanting to transfer some risk, getting a faster start, or the outside cost simply being lower. Reasons to make include protecting confidential knowledge, building the organisation's own capability, keeping control over quality or timing, or the work being core to what the organisation does.

The decision should compare total costs, including the cost of managing a supplier, which is often underestimated. It should also consider what happens after the project: buying a system may create long-term dependence on one vendor for support and changes, a form of **lock-in** that should be weighed at the start.

## What a contract is
A **contract** is a mutually binding agreement that obliges the seller to provide the specified product, service or result and obliges the buyer to pay for it. For a contract to be enforceable it generally needs an offer, acceptance, consideration (something of value exchanged), parties with the capacity to contract, and a lawful purpose. Details vary between legal systems, which is why significant contracts involve legal and procurement specialists.

Contracts allocate **risk**: who bears the cost if things take longer or cost more than expected, if requirements change, or if something goes wrong. The choice of contract type is largely a choice about how to share that risk.

## Contract types
Contract types fall into three families, with variations in each.

**Fixed-price contracts** set a total price for a defined scope. The seller bears most of the cost risk: if the work costs more, the seller absorbs it. The buyer gets price certainty but must define the scope well, because changes become new negotiations, often expensive ones.

- **Firm fixed price** (FFP): one price for the work, regardless of the seller's costs. The most common and simplest.
- **Fixed price incentive fee** (FPIF): a target price with a formula that shares cost overruns or underruns between buyer and seller, up to a ceiling price.
- **Fixed price with economic price adjustment** (FP-EPA): a fixed price that can be adjusted for defined external changes, such as inflation or commodity prices, useful for long contracts.

**Cost-reimbursable contracts** pay the seller's legitimate actual costs, plus a fee for profit. The buyer bears most of the cost risk. These suit work where the scope cannot be well defined in advance, such as research and development, but they need careful auditing of costs and give the seller little natural incentive to economise.

- **Cost plus fixed fee** (CPFF): costs plus a fixed fee that does not change with actual costs.
- **Cost plus incentive fee** (CPIF): costs plus a fee that varies with performance against target costs, sharing savings or overruns.
- **Cost plus award fee** (CPAF): costs plus a fee awarded at the buyer's judgement based on subjective performance criteria.

**Time and materials** contracts (T&M) pay agreed rates for time and materials used. They are a hybrid: open-ended in total cost like cost-reimbursable contracts, but with fixed unit rates like fixed-price ones. They suit staff augmentation, small tasks and work whose scope cannot be defined quickly. Buyers usually add a **not-to-exceed** limit to cap the total.

## Incentive contracts and the point of total assumption
Incentive contracts try to align the seller's interests with the buyer's by sharing the gains or losses relative to a target. A fixed price incentive fee contract typically specifies a **target cost**, a **target profit** (which together give the target price), a **share ratio** for overruns and underruns, and a **ceiling price**, the most the buyer will pay.

Suppose the target cost is 100,000, the target profit 10,000, and so the target price 110,000. The ceiling price is 120,000 and the share ratio 70/30, meaning the buyer bears 70 per cent of any overrun and the seller 30 per cent. If actual costs come in at 90,000, the 10,000 saving is shared: the seller's profit rises by 3,000 to 13,000, and the buyer pays 103,000. If costs come in at 105,000, the seller's profit falls by 1,500 to 8,500 and the buyer pays 113,500.

As costs rise, the buyer's payments rise towards the ceiling. The **point of total assumption** (PTA) is the cost at which the ceiling is reached; beyond it, the seller bears every additional cost in full. It is calculated as PTA = target cost + (ceiling price − target price) ÷ buyer's share ratio. In the example, PTA = 100,000 + (120,000 − 110,000) ÷ 0.7 ≈ 114,286. Above that cost, the seller absorbs all overruns, and its profit shrinks rapidly.

Incentive contracts work best when the seller can genuinely influence the outcome and the target is realistic. A target set too low simply turns an incentive contract into a fixed-price contract with extra arithmetic.

## Procurement documents
Buying begins with documents that describe what is wanted and ask sellers to respond.

- A **statement of work** (SOW) describes the work, product or service to be procured in enough detail for prospective sellers to decide whether they can provide it. A good statement of work is clear, complete and focused on what is needed rather than how to do it, unless the how matters.
- A **request for information** (RFI) asks sellers for general information about their capabilities, often to understand the market before a formal procurement.
- A **request for proposal** (RFP) asks sellers to propose how they would meet a need, typically for complex work where approaches differ and price is not the only criterion.
- A **request for quotation** (RFQ) asks for prices for well-defined goods or services.
- An **invitation for bid** (IFB) is similar to a request for quotation, often used in public procurement where price is the main criterion.

Procurement documents also state the **source selection criteria** that will be used to evaluate responses, such as understanding of the need, technical approach, experience, financial stability, management approach, past performance and price. Stating the criteria in advance makes the evaluation fair and defensible, and helps sellers write useful proposals.

## Selecting a seller
Selection often includes a **bidder conference**, a meeting where all prospective sellers can ask questions and hear the same answers, which keeps the process fair. Proposals are then evaluated against the published criteria, sometimes by a panel that scores each one independently, sometimes with an **independent estimate** of what the work should cost to check whether bids are realistic. Shortlisted sellers may present or be interviewed, and the chosen seller's terms are finalised through negotiation.

Public bodies usually operate under strict procurement rules designed to ensure fairness and value for public money. Private organisations have more freedom, but fair, transparent processes still tend to produce better results and fewer disputes.

## Running a contract
Signing the contract is the beginning, not the end. **Contract administration** manages the relationship and the obligations of both parties throughout the work.

- **Monitoring performance** against the contract: deliverables, milestones, quality, reports.
- **Managing changes** through the change procedure the contract defines. Changes made informally, through a conversation with a seller's engineer, can create obligations nobody approved.
- **Inspections and audits** to verify the work and the costs charged.
- **Payments** according to the agreed terms, often tied to milestones or accepted deliverables.
- **Handling claims and disputes.** A **claim** is a request by one party, usually the seller, for compensation or adjustment, often over disputed changes. Most contracts specify how disputes are resolved: negotiation first, then mediation, arbitration or the courts. Negotiated settlement is almost always cheaper.
- **Keeping records** of correspondence, decisions and performance, which become essential if a dispute arises.

**Closing a procurement** involves confirming that all deliverables have been accepted, settling final payments and open claims, returning any property, archiving records, and recording lessons about the seller and the process. A contract can also be terminated early, for convenience or for default, under the conditions it specifies.

## The relationship behind the contract
Contracts work best when both parties treat them as a framework for collaboration rather than a weapon. Long, adversarial negotiations produce contracts that protect each side from the other but leave the project with suppliers who do the letter of the agreement and nothing more. Many practitioners advocate relational approaches: shared objectives, open-book cost information, joint risk registers, regular performance reviews and a willingness to solve problems together. The contract still matters, because it is what both sides fall back on when things go wrong, but the relationship determines how often they need to.

## Contracts for adaptive work
Traditional fixed-price, fixed-scope contracts fit adaptive delivery poorly, because adaptive work expects scope to change. Several alternatives have emerged.

- **Capped time and materials**: the buyer pays for time used, up to a ceiling, and keeps the right to stop at the end of any iteration.
- **Fixed price per iteration**, with scope chosen iteration by iteration by the buyer's product owner.
- **Incremental delivery contracts**, with payments tied to working increments and a right to stop when enough value has been delivered.
- **Exchange clauses**, sometimes summarised as "change for free": the buyer can swap backlog items of similar size without renegotiation, as long as total scope stays the same.
- **Early termination clauses**, sometimes called "money for nothing": the buyer can stop early, paying the seller a fraction of the remaining value, when the remaining backlog is not worth the cost.

These approaches shift the conversation from "did you deliver what we specified?" to "are we getting value for what we are spending?", which suits uncertain work much better.

> example: Northfield bought the booking software platform from a vendor and contracted a small local firm to build the patient-facing screens. For the platform, a firm fixed-price licence with a support agreement made sense: the product was standard and well defined. For the screens, Amira argued against a fixed-price contract, because nobody yet knew exactly what patients needed. The trust agreed a time and materials contract with two-week iterations, a not-to-exceed limit, and a right to stop at the end of any iteration. The supplier was paid fairly for changes that came from learning, and the trust kept control over what was built.

> exam: Expect questions on contract types and who bears the risk (fixed price: seller; cost-reimbursable: buyer; time and materials: in between), the PTA formula and incentive calculations, the purpose of procurement documents (statement of work, request for proposal, request for quotation, invitation for bid), source selection criteria and bidder conferences, and contract change control. Scenario questions often test that changes go through the contract's formal change process and that the project manager works through the procurement function rather than making informal promises to sellers.

## Key ideas
- Make-or-buy decisions weigh skills, cost, control, risk and long-term dependence.
- Contracts allocate risk. Fixed price puts it on the seller, cost-reimbursable on the buyer, time and materials in between.
- Incentive contracts share overruns and savings; the point of total assumption is where the seller starts bearing all extra cost.
- Procurement documents describe the need and the selection criteria; fair, transparent selection reduces disputes.
- Contract administration manages performance, changes, payments, claims and records until formal closure.
- Adaptive work needs contracts that allow scope to change and let the buyer stop when value runs out.

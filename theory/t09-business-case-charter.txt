title: Starting well: business case and charter
summary: How a project earns the right to start: the need, the options, the business case, the charter that authorises it, and the first conversations that set it up to succeed.
why: More projects are doomed in their first few weeks than in their last few months. A project that starts without a clear reason, without real authority, or with promises nobody could keep will struggle however well it is run afterwards. The early documents are often treated as paperwork to get through. They are better understood as the moment when the organisation decides what it is really asking for.

## From need to project
Projects begin with a need: a problem, an opportunity or an obligation. The booking lines are overwhelmed; a competitor has launched a cheaper service; a new law takes effect next year. Before anyone decides to run a project, someone should understand that need well enough to describe it clearly, including who has it, why it matters, and what happens if nothing is done.

This early work is sometimes called a **needs assessment** or business analysis. It asks what the current situation is, what the desired situation is, and what stands between them. It is easy to skip, because the solution often seems obvious from the start. "We need a new booking system" sounds like a need, but it is already a solution. The underlying need might be "patients cannot get through to book appointments", and there may be several ways to meet it: more phone staff, a call-back service, better scheduling, or an online system.

Separating the need from the solution keeps options open. It also gives the project a test it can use throughout: does this decision help meet the need?

## The business case
A **business case** is the documented argument for investing in a project. It sets out the need, the options considered, the recommended option, its expected costs and benefits, its risks, and why it is worth doing. Its audience is the people who must decide whether to fund the project, and its job is to let them make that decision well.

A good business case usually covers the following.

- **The problem or opportunity**, in terms the decision-makers recognise, with evidence of its size.
- **Strategic fit**: how the project supports the organisation's objectives.
- **Options**, always including doing nothing and doing the minimum, with an honest comparison.
- **The recommended option**, and why.
- **Benefits**, how they will be measured, who will own them and when they are expected.
- **Costs**, including running costs after the project, not just the cost of delivery.
- **Timescale**, at the level of detail that is honestly known.
- **Risks**, including the major uncertainties in the estimates themselves.
- **Financial appraisal**, using measures the organisation trusts, such as payback, net present value or benefit-cost ratio (Chapter 4).
- **Assumptions and constraints** on which the case depends.

The business case is not finished when the project is approved. It remains the reference against which the project is judged, and it should be revisited at every phase gate. If costs rise, benefits shrink or the strategy changes, the business case may stop being true. A project that continues after its business case has failed is spending money without a reason.

> myth: "The business case is written to get approval, and then filed." When that happens, nobody notices when the reason for the project disappears. The business case should be a living document, owned by the sponsor and checked at every gate.

## Optimism and the outside view
Early estimates in business cases are notoriously optimistic. The causes are partly psychological and partly political. The planning fallacy, described by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, is the tendency to forecast based on a best-case scenario of how this particular project will go, ignoring how similar projects have actually gone. Bent Flyvbjerg has added the idea of strategic misrepresentation: people sometimes understate costs and overstate benefits deliberately, because that is how projects get approved.

One remedy is to take the **outside view**: instead of building the estimate up from the specifics of this project alone, start from the actual outcomes of a reference class of similar projects. If comparable systems have typically cost forty per cent more than planned and taken half as long again, that is the best starting forecast, adjusted for genuine differences. Flyvbjerg calls this **reference class forecasting**, and several governments now require it for large projects. Even where formal data is lacking, asking "how did similar projects actually go?" corrects a great deal of optimism.

## The charter
Once the organisation decides to proceed, the project needs to be formally authorised. The document that does this is usually called the **project charter**. It is issued by the sponsor or another person with the authority to commit the organisation's resources.

The charter does three essential things. It **formally authorises** the project to exist and to use the organisation's resources. It **names the project manager** and states their level of authority. And it **records the high-level agreement** about what the project is for and what success looks like, so that everyone starts from the same understanding.

Typical contents include:

- the purpose and justification, linked to the business case;
- measurable objectives and success criteria;
- high-level requirements and a description of the product;
- the main boundaries: what is included, and what is not;
- summary milestones and a summary budget;
- key stakeholders;
- known high-level risks, assumptions and constraints;
- the project manager's responsibility and authority;
- the sponsor's name and authority, and the approval.

A charter is deliberately short. It is not a plan; the plan comes later, built on the charter's foundations. Its value lies in the agreement it records. Writing it forces the sponsor and the project manager to have conversations that are easy to postpone: what exactly are we trying to achieve, how will we know we have succeeded, what is out of bounds, and how much authority does the project manager really have?

## Assumptions and constraints
Every early plan rests on **assumptions**, things believed to be true but not yet confirmed, and **constraints**, limits the project must work within. "The records vendor will deliver the interface by March" is an assumption. "The system must comply with data protection law" and "the budget cannot exceed two million" are constraints.

Both need to be written down, because both are sources of risk. An assumption that turns out to be false can undermine the whole plan; a constraint that was not understood can make the plan impossible. Recording them in an **assumption log** gives the team a list to check regularly. When an assumption is confirmed, it can be closed. When it looks shaky, it should be treated as a risk.

## Identifying stakeholders early
Stakeholder identification begins before the project is formally under way. The sponsor, the main users, the teams who will support the result, regulators, suppliers and anyone whose work will change all need to be found early, because the cost of discovering an important stakeholder late is high. A group that learns about a project when it is nearly finished can delay or derail it, and may be right to: they may know something the project needed.

Early identification does not need to be elaborate. A brainstorm with the sponsor and a few knowledgeable people, followed by a simple list of who is affected and how, is enough to begin. Chapter 18 discusses stakeholder engagement in detail.

## Starting an adaptive project
Adaptive projects need the same foundations, though they often express them differently. Instead of a detailed scope statement, they need a clear **product vision**: who the product is for, what problem it solves, and why it matters. Many teams capture this in a short vision statement or a one-page vision board.

Adaptive teams also start with a **team charter** or set of **working agreements**: how the team will work together, make decisions, communicate and handle disagreement, and what its definition of done is. These agreements are created by the team itself, which is part of their value.

Some organisations begin adaptive work with a short **inception** or discovery period: a few days or weeks to clarify the vision, identify the main users and their needs, sketch the architecture, build an initial backlog, and identify the biggest risks. Inception is not a disguised big design up front. Its aim is to learn just enough to start delivering sensibly.

> example: Northfield's sponsor was Dr Okafor, the trust's director of outpatient services. Her first draft of the charter said the project would "implement an online booking system". Amira asked her what would count as success. After some discussion, the objectives became: at least forty per cent of routine bookings made online within a year of rollout, the average wait to get through on the phone below five minutes, and no increase in missed appointments. The scope excluded specialist referrals in the first release. These few sentences later settled several arguments about what the project was for.

## The kick-off
The **kick-off meeting** brings the team and key stakeholders together at the start to establish a shared understanding. It typically covers the purpose and objectives, the approach and main milestones, roles and responsibilities, how the team will communicate and make decisions, and the major risks. On large projects there may be separate kick-offs for the sponsor and governance, the core team, and wider stakeholders.

The kick-off matters less for the information it conveys, which could be written down, than for what it establishes socially: that the project is real, that the sponsor is committed, that people know who their colleagues are, and that questions and concerns are welcome. A sponsor who speaks at the kick-off, explains why the project matters and stays to answer questions sends a signal no document can.

## Common mistakes at the start
Several early mistakes recur.

- **Starting before the purpose is agreed.** The team begins work while the sponsor and key stakeholders still disagree about what the project is for.
- **A charter with no real authority.** The project manager is named but has no say over people or money, and nobody has said so.
- **Success defined only as delivery.** Objectives are about outputs and dates, with nothing about the outcome the organisation needs.
- **Committing to precise numbers too early.** A date and budget are announced before anyone has done enough work to estimate them, and the project spends its life explaining the variance.
- **Ignoring the people who must live with the result.** Operations, support teams and end users are not involved until handover.

Each of these is far cheaper to fix in the first weeks than later.

> exam: Expect questions about what the charter does (authorises the project and the project manager), who issues it (the sponsor or someone with authority to commit resources), and what to do when there is none (obtain one before proceeding). Questions about the business case often test whether you revisit it when circumstances change. In adaptive scenarios, look for the product vision, the team charter and early backlog creation rather than a detailed scope statement.

## Key ideas
- Start from the need, not the solution, and keep the option of doing nothing in view.
- The business case argues for the investment and remains the reference for whether the project is still worth doing.
- Early estimates are optimistic; the outside view, starting from how similar projects actually went, corrects much of that.
- The charter authorises the project, names the project manager and their authority, and records the high-level agreement on purpose and success.
- Assumptions and constraints should be written down early and checked often.
- Adaptive projects start with a product vision, team working agreements and often a short inception.

title: Communication that works
summary: Why communication dominates the project manager's day, how messages get lost, choosing the right method and channel, and the habits of communication that build trust.
why: Most project problems are, at some level, communication problems: a requirement understood differently by two people, a risk someone noticed and nobody heard, a decision made in one meeting and unknown in another. Communication is not a soft extra around the real work. On a project, it is much of the real work.

## Why communication is so hard on projects
Projects bring together people who often have not worked together, come from different disciplines and organisations, use different vocabularies, and are under time pressure. Each of these makes communication harder. Engineers, clinicians, finance staff and vendors may use the same word to mean different things. Team members may be in different places, time zones and cultures. And the number of people who need to stay aligned grows quickly as a project grows.

That last point has a simple mathematical form. The number of **communication channels** between n people is n(n − 1) ÷ 2. Five people have 10 channels; ten people have 45; twenty have 190. Each new person adds not one channel but as many as there are people already involved. This is one reason large projects need more structure for communication than small ones, and why splitting work into small, stable teams helps.

## How messages get lost
A classic model describes communication as a **sender** who **encodes** an idea into a message, transmits it through a **medium**, and a **receiver** who **decodes** it. **Noise**, anything that interferes with the message, can distort it at any stage: jargon, distraction, language differences, emotions, assumptions, a bad phone line. The receiver's **feedback** tells the sender whether the message arrived as intended.

The model's main lesson is that a message sent is not a message received, and a message received is not a message understood. Responsibility for communication is shared: the sender must make the message clear and check it was understood; the receiver must listen, ask and confirm. Many project misunderstandings would be avoided by one simple habit: asking "what did you understand me to say?"

Several barriers are especially common on projects.

- **Jargon and acronyms** that one group takes for granted and another does not know.
- **Assumed context**: the sender knows the background and forgets the receiver does not.
- **Filtering**: bad news softened as it travels up the hierarchy, until it arrives as good news.
- **Channel mismatch**: a sensitive or complex message sent by email when it needed a conversation.
- **Overload**: so many messages that important ones are lost.
- **Silence**: people who do not speak up because they fear the response.

## Choosing a method
Communication methods are often grouped into three kinds.

- **Interactive communication** happens between two or more people in real time: meetings, calls, video conferences, conversations. It is the best way to deal with complex, sensitive or ambiguous matters, because misunderstandings can be cleared up immediately.
- **Push communication** is sent to specific recipients: emails, reports, memos, messages. It ensures delivery but not reading or understanding.
- **Pull communication** is placed where people can access it when they choose: shared documents, intranet sites, knowledge bases, dashboards. It suits large audiences and reference information.

The choice depends on the message, the audience and the urgency. A schedule slip that affects a director's commitment to her board deserves a conversation, followed by an email confirming what was agreed. A weekly status summary for fifty people is better pulled from a dashboard. A design question between two engineers is best settled at a whiteboard or on a call.

Alistair Cockburn, writing about software teams, observed that the effectiveness of communication rises with **richness**: two people at a whiteboard communicate more, faster, than two people on a phone, who communicate more than two people exchanging documents. This is why agile principles emphasise face-to-face conversation. Richer channels cost more time per person, so the art is to use rich channels for the communication that needs them and lighter channels for the rest.

## Planning communication
On larger projects, a **communications management plan** records who needs what information, when, in what form, from whom, and through which channel. It typically covers the main stakeholder groups, the regular reports and meetings, escalation paths, how sensitive information is handled, and any glossary of terms.

The most useful plans start from the needs of the audience rather than from what the project wants to say. A sponsor needs a short view of progress against objectives, emerging risks and decisions required. A team member needs detail about the work and its dependencies. A regulator may need formal documentation in a prescribed format. A plan that sends everyone the same report serves nobody well.

## Meetings
Meetings are where much project communication happens, and where much project time is wasted. A few practices make them worth their cost.

- **A clear purpose.** Is the meeting to share information, solve a problem, or make a decision? Each needs a different design.
- **An agenda**, circulated in advance, with the time allotted for each item.
- **The right people**: those needed for the purpose, and no more.
- **A timebox**, respected.
- **Recorded outcomes**: decisions and actions, with owners and dates, sent promptly.
- **The question: could this be done without a meeting?**

Adaptive teams formalise meetings into a small set of events with clear purposes and strict timeboxes: planning, a short daily stand-up, review and retrospective. The daily stand-up in particular is often misused as a status report to a manager; its purpose is for the team to coordinate its own work for the next day.

## Reporting
Project reports are a form of push communication with a specific purpose: to give decision-makers the information they need to act. Chapter 17 discussed what to measure. On the communication side, good reports share some traits.

- **Lead with what matters**: the overall state, what has changed, and what decisions are needed.
- **Be honest.** Reports that are optimistic until the moment of failure destroy trust in every future report.
- **Explain, do not just present.** A variance without a cause and a proposed response is half a report.
- **Fit the audience.** Executives need less detail and more implication; teams need more detail.
- **Be consistent**, so readers can compare one report with the last.

Visual tools, such as charts, boards and dashboards, are often clearer than text. Adaptive teams rely heavily on **information radiators**, displays that make the work visible to anyone who looks, which reduce the need for reporting altogether.

## Listening
Most writing on communication focuses on sending. For project managers, listening may matter more. **Active listening** means giving full attention, not preparing your reply while the other person speaks, asking questions to understand, and summarising what you heard to check it. It sounds simple and is rare in practice.

Listening is how a project manager learns what is really happening: the concern a team member mentions in passing, the hesitation in a stakeholder's voice, the problem a supplier hints at but does not state. It is also how trust is built. People who feel heard are more willing to raise problems early, which is exactly what a project needs.

## Communicating across distance and culture
Many projects involve people in different locations, organisations and cultures. Distance removes much of the informal communication that keeps co-located teams aligned, the overheard conversation, the quick question, the shared lunch. Distributed teams need to replace it deliberately: regular video calls with cameras on, shared online boards, written working agreements, overlap hours across time zones, and occasional in-person meetings, especially at the start.

Cultural differences affect communication in ways that are easy to misread. Cultures differ in how directly they express disagreement, how they treat hierarchy, how much context they expect to be understood without being said, and how they view time and deadlines. A team member who never says "no" may be signalling a serious problem through hints that the project manager does not recognise. The remedy is curiosity rather than stereotypes: learning how each person prefers to communicate, making expectations explicit, and checking understanding more often.

> example: Northfield's weekly status email went to more than eighty people and was read by almost none of them. Amira replaced it with three things: a one-page dashboard on the trust intranet, updated weekly, which anyone could check; a ten-minute fortnightly briefing for clinic managers, where they could ask questions; and a direct call to the sponsor whenever a risk crossed an agreed threshold. Questions to the project team dropped, and clinic managers said for the first time that they knew what was happening.

> myth: "If it's in the email, they've been told." Sending is not communicating. If something matters, check that it has been understood, and choose a channel that fits its importance.

> exam: Expect the communication channels formula, n(n − 1) ÷ 2, and questions where the number of people changes. Know the sender-receiver model, the three methods (interactive, push and pull) and when each fits, and the role of the communications management plan. Scenario questions often reward choosing interactive communication for sensitive or complex issues, tailoring communication to the audience, and making sure messages were understood.

## Key ideas
- Communication is much of a project manager's work; the number of channels grows rapidly with team size.
- A message sent is not a message understood. Senders check understanding; receivers listen and confirm.
- Interactive communication suits complex and sensitive matters; push ensures delivery; pull suits large audiences and reference material.
- Richer channels convey more; use them for what needs them.
- Plan communication from the audience's needs, keep meetings purposeful and timeboxed, and report honestly.
- Listening builds trust and surfaces problems early; distance and culture call for deliberate, explicit practices.

summary: How groups become teams, what motivates people in knowledge work, how to develop and look after a team, and how teams decide and work together.
why: Projects are delivered by teams, and the difference between a group of capable individuals and a real team is enormous. A real team shares a goal, trusts its members, solves problems together and holds itself accountable. Such teams are not assembled; they are built, often in difficult conditions: temporary, part-time, distributed and under pressure. Knowing how teams form and what makes them work is one of the most practical skills a project manager can have.

## Groups and teams
A **group** is a set of people working in the same place or on related tasks. A **team** is a small number of people with complementary skills who are committed to a common purpose, shared goals and an approach for which they hold themselves mutually accountable. The definition, from Jon Katzenbach and Douglas Smith, highlights what turns a group into a team: a purpose everyone shares, goals that require working together, and accountability to each other rather than only to a manager.

Not every project needs every group to become a team in this full sense. A collection of specialists contributing independently may be fine for simple work. But where the work requires people to combine their knowledge, solve problems together and adapt to change, which is most project work, the difference between a group and a team shows directly in the results.

## How teams develop
Bruce Tuckman's model, proposed in 1965 and extended in 1977, describes stages that groups often pass through.

- **Forming**: people come together, are polite and uncertain, and look to the leader for direction.
- **Storming**: differences emerge about goals, roles and ways of working; conflict and frustration rise.
- **Norming**: the team agrees how it will work; trust and cohesion grow.
- **Performing**: the team works effectively and largely manages itself.
- **Adjourning**: the work ends and the team disbands, which can bring a sense of loss as well as achievement.

[figure: t20-tuckman]

The model is a useful lens rather than a strict sequence. Teams can skip or repeat stages, and a change of membership, goals or circumstances can send a performing team back to storming. Its practical value lies in two lessons. First, storming is normal and often necessary: a team that never disagrees may never have engaged with the hard questions. Second, the leader's role changes with the stage: more direction early on, more facilitation through storming and norming, more delegation once the team is performing.

## What makes teams effective
Research on teams points to a few conditions that matter more than individual talent.

- **A clear, compelling purpose** that the team understands and cares about.
- **Psychological safety**: the shared belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks, such as asking questions, admitting mistakes or disagreeing. Amy Edmondson's research, and Google's study of its own teams, found it to be among the strongest predictors of team effectiveness (Chapter 5).
- **Dependability**: members do what they say they will.
- **Clear roles and goals**, so people know what is expected of them and of each other.
- **Meaning and impact**: members believe their work matters.
- **The right size.** Small teams, often cited as five to nine people, communicate and coordinate far more easily than large ones.
- **Stability.** Teams whose membership changes constantly never get past forming.

Patrick Lencioni described the reverse in his model of five dysfunctions, each building on the one below: absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, and inattention to results. A team that cannot trust its members avoids honest conflict; without honest debate, members do not truly commit; without commitment, they do not hold each other accountable; and without accountability, individual interests displace the team's results.

## Motivation
Why do people work hard on a project, especially one where the project manager does not control their pay or promotion? Several theories offer partial answers, and together they are useful.

**Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs** suggests that people are motivated by needs in a rough order: physiological, safety, belonging, esteem and self-actualisation. Higher needs motivate once lower ones are reasonably met. For project teams, the practical point is that people worried about job security will not be motivated by interesting work until that worry is addressed.

**Frederick Herzberg's two-factor theory** distinguishes **hygiene factors**, such as pay, working conditions, policies and relationships with supervisors, from **motivators**, such as achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility and growth. Poor hygiene factors cause dissatisfaction, but good ones do not motivate; they only prevent dissatisfaction. Motivation comes from the motivators.

**Douglas McGregor** described two sets of assumptions managers hold. **Theory X** assumes people dislike work, avoid responsibility and need to be controlled. **Theory Y** assumes people find work natural, seek responsibility and are self-directed when committed to goals. The assumptions tend to be self-fulfilling: people treated as Theory X workers behave like them.

**Victor Vroom's expectancy theory** says people are motivated when they believe their effort will lead to performance, that performance will lead to an outcome, and that the outcome is something they value.

**Daniel Pink**, drawing on research by Edward Deci, Richard Ryan and others, argues that for complex, creative work, the strongest motivators are **autonomy** (control over how one works), **mastery** (getting better at something that matters) and **purpose** (working towards something bigger than oneself). External rewards such as bonuses can work for simple, routine tasks but often reduce motivation and creativity for complex ones.

For project managers without control of pay or promotion, these theories point in the same direction: give people meaningful work, a clear purpose, real responsibility, room to decide how to do their work, recognition for their contribution, and chances to learn. These are within a project manager's gift.

## Developing the team
Team development is an ongoing activity, not an event at the start. It includes:

- **Building shared understanding** of the goal, the approach and each other's roles, often through a kick-off and a team charter or working agreements.
- **Training and coaching**, both in skills the project needs and in ways of working.
- **Recognition**: noticing and appreciating good work, specifically and promptly. Recognition costs little and is one of the most powerful motivators available.
- **Team-building**: not only social events, but working together on real problems, which builds trust faster than any exercise.
- **Co-location**, where possible, or deliberate practices that compensate for distance.
- **Regular reflection**: retrospectives and lessons learned, so the team improves how it works.

**Emotional intelligence**, the ability to recognise and manage one's own emotions and to recognise and respond to others', underpins much of this. Daniel Goleman popularised the idea and argued that it predicts leadership effectiveness at least as well as technical skill. For project managers, it shows in practical ways: staying calm under pressure, noticing when a team member is struggling, handling criticism without defensiveness, and reading the mood of a meeting.

## Self-organising and cross-functional teams
Adaptive approaches emphasise **self-organising teams**: teams that decide for themselves how to do their work, within the goals and constraints set for them. Self-organisation does not mean no structure or no leadership. It means the people closest to the work make the decisions about how to do it, because they know most about it, and that leadership focuses on setting direction and removing obstacles.

They also emphasise **cross-functional teams**, which contain all the skills needed to deliver a working result, rather than handing work between specialist departments. Each hand-off is a source of delay, misunderstanding and lost knowledge; a cross-functional team removes many of them. Team members are often described as **T-shaped**: deep expertise in one area and enough breadth to help in others, which makes the team more resilient and flexible.

## Looking after the team
Projects often put people under pressure, and sustained pressure is costly. Overtime produces short-term gains and long-term losses: fatigue increases errors, reduces judgement and drives good people away. The agile principle of a **sustainable pace**, working at a rate the team could keep up indefinitely, reflects long experience that heroic effort is a symptom of a planning problem rather than a solution.

Looking after the team also means managing workload fairly, protecting people from unnecessary interruptions and context switching, dealing promptly with poor behaviour, and planning for what happens to people when the project ends. A project that wears out its team may deliver on time, but the organisation pays for it in turnover and in every project that follows.

> example: Northfield's booking team included developers from the supplier, two trust IT staff and a part-time clinical adviser. In the first month, the supplier's developers and the trust's staff worked in separate rooms and communicated through tickets; progress was slow and blame frequent. Amira moved everyone into one room three days a week, ran a session where the team wrote its own working agreements, and asked the clinical adviser to bring two patients to the first sprint review. Seeing real patients struggle with an early screen did more for the team's sense of shared purpose than any meeting about it.

> myth: "Self-organising teams don't need a leader." They need a different kind of leadership: one that sets direction, protects the team, removes obstacles and coaches, rather than assigning tasks. Teams left without any leadership often drift, avoid conflict or fall back on the loudest voice.

> exam: Expect questions on the Tuckman stages, the motivation theories (Maslow, Herzberg, McGregor, Vroom, and the autonomy-mastery-purpose view), emotional intelligence, team charters, recognition and co-location or virtual team practices. Scenario questions often reward supporting the team to solve its own problems, addressing conflict and performance issues directly but privately, recognising contributions, and protecting the team from distractions. In adaptive scenarios, the team decides how to do the work.

## Key ideas
- A team shares a purpose, goals and mutual accountability; a group may not.
- Teams typically move through forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning; leadership should adapt to the stage.
- Psychological safety, dependability, clear goals, meaning, small size and stability make teams effective.
- Motivation for knowledge work comes from purpose, autonomy, mastery, recognition and meaningful work more than from pay alone.
- Team development is continuous: shared agreements, coaching, recognition, working together and reflection.
- Self-organising, cross-functional teams decide how to work; sustainable pace protects them and the organisation.

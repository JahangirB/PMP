summary: What makes work a project, how projects differ from the running of an organisation, and why value, not output, is the point.
why: Every method in this discipline, from a Gantt chart to a sprint review, exists because projects are a particular kind of work with particular problems. If you are clear about what a project is and what it is for, most of the practices in later chapters stop looking like rules to memorise and start looking like sensible answers to those problems.

## Two kinds of work
Every organisation does two kinds of work. The first keeps it running: the payroll is paid each month, the call centre answers calls, the factory makes this week's orders, the ward is staffed tonight. This is **operations**. It repeats, it is measured by how steadily and efficiently it repeats, and the people doing it are usually organised in permanent departments.

The second kind of work changes the organisation. A new product is designed, a building goes up, a computer system is replaced, a merger is carried through, a law is brought into effect. This is **project** work. It is done once, it ends, and when it ends something exists that did not exist before.

The most common definition says a project is a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service or result. Each word in that sentence carries weight, and each one points to a problem that project management has to solve.

## Temporary does not mean short
"Temporary" means the project has a beginning and an end. It does not mean it is quick. Building a railway can take fifteen years and is still temporary, because one day the line opens and the project team disbands. The end can come in several ways: the objectives are met, it becomes clear they cannot be met, the need disappears, the money or the people run out, or the sponsor decides to stop.

Temporariness has consequences that are easy to underestimate. The team is assembled for the work and will scatter afterwards, so it has to form, agree how it works, and become productive quickly, without the years of shared habit a permanent department enjoys. Knowledge gathered during the project leaves with the people unless someone deliberately captures it. And because there is an end, there is always a question of who will own the result once the project has gone. A surprising number of projects deliver something that nobody in operations was ready to receive.

What is temporary is the project, not its output. The railway, the software or the new process is meant to last for years. A project manager who forgets this optimises the wrong thing: finishing on the planned date with a result that is expensive to run, hard to maintain or unloved by the people who must use it every day.

## Unique, but rarely new in every part
"Unique" means the result has not been produced before in exactly this form, in this place, for these people. It does not mean that everything about it is new. A housing developer may have built a hundred similar houses, but this site has its own ground conditions, its own neighbours and its own planning officer. A software team may have shipped many releases, but this one carries a different set of changes into a system that has itself changed since the last time.

Uniqueness is the source of uncertainty. Operations can be standardised because the same thing happens again and again; the organisation learns its true costs and durations from experience. A project can borrow from past experience, but never completely, because some part of it has not happened before. That is why estimates on projects are ranges rather than facts, why risks have to be looked for rather than simply listed from last time, and why a project plan is always partly a hypothesis about how the work will go.

The degree of uniqueness varies enormously. Repeating a well-understood kind of project in a familiar setting carries little uncertainty and can be planned in detail. Building something nobody has built before, for users whose needs nobody fully understands, carries a great deal. Much of the modern debate about predictive and adaptive approaches (Part II) is really a debate about how much uniqueness, and therefore how much uncertainty, a piece of work contains.

## Progressive elaboration
Because a project creates something partly new, nobody knows everything about it at the start. Understanding grows as the work proceeds. Early on, the project may be described in a page: the purpose, the rough scope, an indication of cost and time. Later the description becomes a detailed design, a schedule of individual activities, a budget broken down by work package. This deepening of detail as knowledge improves is called **progressive elaboration**.

Progressive elaboration is not the same as uncontrolled change. The purpose of the project should stay stable while the detail of how to achieve it fills in. A health trust that sets out to let patients book appointments online should still be doing that at the end; what changes is the understanding of which appointments, which screens, which integrations and which edge cases. When the purpose itself shifts, that is a different matter, and it usually calls for a decision by whoever authorised the project in the first place.

A related idea is **rolling wave planning**: planning the near term in detail and the further future in outline, then adding detail as each period approaches. It is simply progressive elaboration applied to the schedule. Teams using adaptive approaches do something similar with their backlog, refining the items they will work on soon and leaving the rest coarse.

> example: Northfield Health Trust runs three hospitals and forty community clinics. Patients currently book appointments by phone, and the booking lines are overwhelmed every morning. The trust's board has approved a project to let patients book, move and cancel appointments online. At the start, the whole project is described in a two-page business case. By month three there is a list of more than two hundred requirements; by month six, the first clinics are using a working version. We will follow this project through Part I.

## Projects as the vehicle of change
Why do organisations run projects at all? Because projects are how organisations change on purpose. Strategy is a statement about where an organisation wants to be; operations keep it where it is; projects move it from one state to the other.

Projects are usually triggered by one of a handful of pressures. A market demand (customers want online booking). A strategic opportunity (a new service line). A customer request (a client commissions a bespoke system). A technological advance (an old platform can be replaced by a better one). A legal or regulatory requirement (new rules on patient data). A social need (a community needs a clinic). Or simply a problem that cannot be fixed within normal operations (the booking lines fail every morning).

Seeing projects this way changes the questions a project manager asks. The question is not only "are we building the thing we were asked to build?" but "will the organisation be in the better state it was hoping for when we are done?" The first question is about outputs. The second is about value.

## Outputs, outcomes, benefits and value
These four words are often used loosely. It helps to keep them apart.

An **output** is what the project produces: the online booking system, the trained call-centre staff, the updated procedures. Outputs can be inspected and accepted; they either meet their specification or they do not.

An **outcome** is the change that results when the output is used: patients book online instead of phoning, so the morning queue shrinks. Outcomes happen in the organisation and among its customers, not inside the project, and they often appear only after the project has handed over.

A **benefit** is a measurable improvement that someone values: fewer missed appointments, lower call-centre costs, higher patient satisfaction scores. Benefits are the reason the outcome matters.

**Value** is the overall worth of those benefits to the people who care about them, set against what it cost to get them. Value can be financial, but it is often not: safety, compliance, reputation, public good and learning are all forms of value.

The chain matters because the project directly controls only its first link. It can guarantee outputs; it can influence outcomes; it rarely controls benefits on its own. This is why mature organisations keep benefits in view from the business case onwards, assign someone to own them after handover, and judge projects by more than whether the outputs arrived.

> myth: "A project that finishes on time, on budget and to specification is a success." It is a delivery success. Whether it is a business success depends on whether the outputs are used and the benefits arrive. Plenty of systems are delivered perfectly and switched off a year later.

## The iron triangle, and why it is not enough
For decades, project success was described with three constraints: scope, time and cost, often drawn as a triangle with quality in the middle. The idea is sound as far as it goes. The three are linked; you cannot ask for more scope in less time at lower cost without something giving way, and when one moves the others are put under pressure.

The triangle is a useful first model, but practitioners found it too simple. Real projects also juggle risk, the quality expected by different stakeholders, the availability of skilled people, the demands of regulators, the reputation of the organisation and the experience of the people who will use the result. Modern standards therefore talk about competing constraints rather than three, and they place the constraints in service of value rather than treating them as the goal.

There is a deeper problem with the triangle as a definition of success. It describes the project's efficiency, not its effectiveness. A project can stay within all three constraints and still deliver something nobody needed. Conversely, some of the most valuable projects in history overran badly. The Sydney Opera House was finished a decade late and many times over its budget, yet it became one of the most recognised buildings in the world. That is not an argument for overruns, which usually destroy value rather than create it. It is an argument for judging a project by what it was for.

## Projects and products
A **product** is something an organisation offers and sustains over time: a mobile app, a range of cars, an insurance policy, a public service. A product has a life cycle of its own, from idea through development, growth and maturity to retirement. One product may be shaped by many projects across its life: the first build, a major redesign, a move to a new platform, the final decommissioning.

Many organisations, especially in software, have moved from funding projects to funding long-lived product teams. Instead of assembling a team, delivering a fixed scope and disbanding, they keep a stable team working on a product continuously, choosing the most valuable next piece of work each week. This reduces some of the costs of temporariness: knowledge stays in the team, and nobody has to hand the result over to strangers. It does not remove the need for project thinking. Large changes inside a product still have goals, deadlines, budgets, risks and stakeholders, and still benefit from being planned and governed as a piece of work with an end.

In the running case, the online booking system will become a product that the trust maintains for years. The project is the effort that brings its first version into service and gets the clinics using it. Recognising that distinction early led the trust to involve the team that would support the system after go-live in the project itself, so that the handover was a gradual transfer rather than a cliff edge.

## Why projects struggle
Studies of project performance have been published for decades, and their headline figures vary widely depending on how failure is defined. The causes they find, though, are remarkably consistent. They are worth knowing because almost every practice in this field is a response to one of them.

- **Unclear purpose.** The project starts without a shared understanding of why it exists or what success looks like, so every later decision rests on sand.
- **Optimistic estimates.** People forecast the best case and call it the plan. Psychologists call this the planning fallacy, and it affects experts as well as novices.
- **Weak sponsorship.** Nobody senior enough owns the project, so conflicts with other priorities are never resolved in its favour.
- **Requirements that were never understood.** The team builds what was written down, which was not what was needed.
- **Unmanaged change.** The scope drifts one reasonable request at a time until the plan no longer describes the work.
- **Poor stakeholder engagement.** The people affected by the result learn about it too late to shape it, and resist it when it arrives.
- **Risks noticed too late.** Problems that were visible early are discovered when they have become expensive.
- **Teams that never became teams.** People are assigned part-time to work they do not own, and nobody feels accountable for the whole.

> exam: The exam rarely asks for a definition of a project, but it constantly tests the ideas behind it. When a question describes a team building to specification while the business need has changed, the answer usually involves going back to the business case and the sponsor. When it describes benefits that will appear after handover, expect an answer about transition and benefits ownership rather than about closing the project faster.

## The work of managing a project
If a project is a temporary effort to create something partly new in order to deliver value, then managing it means a few things that recur throughout this book. It means establishing and protecting a shared purpose. It means turning that purpose into a plan that is detailed where knowledge allows and open where it does not. It means organising people who may not have worked together into a team that can deliver. It means watching for the gap between plan and reality and deciding what to do about it. It means engaging the people who will be affected, long before the result reaches them. And it means keeping the end in view: not only the end date, but the state the organisation should be in afterwards.

None of these is mechanical. Each requires judgement, and much of the rest of this book is about how experienced practitioners exercise that judgement.

## Key ideas
- A project is a temporary effort to create a unique product, service or result. Operations sustain the organisation; projects change it.
- Temporary means it ends, not that it is short. The output is meant to last.
- Uniqueness creates uncertainty, which is why plans are partly hypotheses and estimates are ranges.
- Progressive elaboration fills in detail as knowledge grows; the purpose should stay stable while it does.
- Outputs are produced by the project, outcomes happen when they are used, benefits are the measurable improvements, and value is their worth set against their cost.
- Scope, time and cost matter, but they serve value. Delivery success and business success are different things.
- Products last; projects end. Many changes to a product are still best run as projects.

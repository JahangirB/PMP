title: The principles behind good practice
summary: Twelve principles that underlie every method, why each one holds, and how to use them when no rule fits.
why: Methods tell you what to do in situations their authors foresaw. Principles tell you how to think in situations nobody foresaw, which on real projects is most of them. They are also the bridge between predictive and adaptive ways of working: the practices differ, but good practitioners in both traditions are following the same principles.

## Why principles rather than processes
For most of its history, project management was taught as processes: do this, then this, produce these documents. Processes are valuable. They capture experience, give teams a shared language and stop important things from being forgotten. But a process is a solution to a problem in a particular context, and when the context changes, following the process faithfully can produce the wrong result.

A principle is a statement of something that is generally true about how good outcomes are achieved. It does not tell you exactly what to do; it tells you what to aim for and what to watch out for. The seventh edition of the PMBOK Guide reorganised the standard around twelve such principles, grouped in no particular order and intended to apply to any project, whatever the approach. This chapter explains each of them in its own terms, with the reasoning behind it. Where useful, it also points to the tensions between them, because much of the judgement in project management lies in balancing principles that pull in different directions.

## 1. Be a diligent, respectful and caring steward
Stewardship means managing something on behalf of others. A project manager is entrusted with other people's money, time, reputations and wellbeing, and with resources that belong to the organisation and sometimes to society. The principle asks you to act accordingly: with integrity, with care for the people affected, with honesty about how things stand, and with attention to obligations that go beyond the project itself, such as safety, the law, and the environment.

Stewardship has a practical side. It is spending money as if it were your own, reporting bad news early, refusing to hide problems from the people who need to know, and leaving things in good order for those who come after. It also has an outward side. Decisions about materials, suppliers or working hours affect people well beyond the project, and a steward considers them.

## 2. Create a collaborative project team environment
Projects are delivered by teams, and teams deliver more when they work together well. This principle asks the project manager to build an environment where people share information, help one another, agree how they will work, and feel safe to raise problems.

The reasoning is straightforward. Project work is too complex for any one person to understand fully, so outcomes depend on knowledge being combined. When people protect their own area, hold back bad news or wait to be told what to do, that combination fails. Research on teams, including Google's well-known study of its own teams, has found that **psychological safety**, the belief that you will not be punished for speaking up, asking questions or admitting mistakes, is one of the strongest predictors of team effectiveness. Team agreements, clear roles, shared goals and a leader who responds well to bad news all help to create it.

## 3. Effectively engage with stakeholders
Stakeholders are the people and groups who affect or are affected by the project. Their support, resistance, knowledge and decisions shape what is possible. The principle asks you to identify them, understand their interests and influence, and engage them actively throughout the project, not just inform them at milestones.

Engagement matters for two reasons. The first is that stakeholders hold information the project needs: what the users actually do, what the regulator will accept, what the operations team can support. The second is that people who have shaped something are far more likely to adopt it than people who have had it imposed on them. A technically excellent result that the people affected reject is not a success.

## 4. Focus on value
Projects exist to deliver value, and the principle asks you to keep that in view continuously, not just in the business case. That means understanding what value the project is meant to create and for whom, checking regularly whether it is still on course to do so, and being prepared to change direction, or stop, when it is not.

Focusing on value changes everyday decisions. It favours delivering the most valuable parts first, so that some benefit arrives even if the project is cut short. It encourages measuring outcomes rather than activity. And it gives the project manager a basis for saying no to scope that adds cost without adding value, however reasonable each individual request may sound.

## 5. Recognise, evaluate and respond to system interactions
A project is a system: many parts that interact with one another and with the organisation and environment around them. Changes in one part produce effects, often delayed and often surprising, in others. **Systems thinking** asks you to look at the whole, not just the parts, and to anticipate those interactions.

A classic example is adding people to a late project. It seems obvious that more people will finish the work sooner. But new people need to be trained by the existing team, the number of communication paths grows rapidly with team size, and the work may not divide neatly. Frederick Brooks observed in 1975 that adding people to a late software project makes it later, which became known as Brooks's law. The specific claim does not hold everywhere, but the point does: in a system, the intuitive fix often makes things worse, because it ignores the interactions.

## 6. Demonstrate leadership behaviours
Leadership is not confined to the person with the title. The principle asks everyone on the project, and especially the project manager, to show leadership: setting direction, motivating others, taking responsibility, influencing without authority, and adapting their style to the situation and the people.

There is no single right style. A team in a crisis may need clear direction; an expert team on novel work may need a leader who asks questions and removes obstacles; a new team may need more structure than a mature one. The principle is about adapting style deliberately, with enough self-awareness to know your default and when it is not working.

## 7. Tailor based on context
Every project is different, so the approach to it should be shaped to fit. Tailoring means deliberately choosing the life cycle, processes, practices, documents and level of formality that suit this project's size, complexity, risk, uncertainty, regulatory setting, team and organisation.

The reasoning cuts both ways. Too little process leaves important things undone and risks unmanaged. Too much wastes effort, slows decisions and buries the real work under ceremony. A ten-person internal improvement project does not need the governance of a national infrastructure programme, and a regulated medical device cannot be run with the informality of a weekend prototype. Tailoring is not a licence to skip what is inconvenient. It is a responsibility to choose consciously, and to be able to explain the choice. Chapter 8 looks at it in detail.

## 8. Build quality into processes and deliverables
Quality means meeting requirements and being fit for the purpose the result is meant to serve. The principle asks you to build it in from the start, in how the work is done, rather than inspect it in at the end.

This idea comes from the quality movement associated with W. Edwards Deming and others, and from lean manufacturing: defects are cheapest to prevent, more expensive to find early, and most expensive to find late or, worst of all, after delivery. On projects, building quality in means clear acceptance criteria agreed before work starts, reviews and testing throughout rather than at the end, definitions of done that every piece of work must meet, and a culture where people are encouraged to stop and fix problems rather than pass them on.

## 9. Navigate complexity
Complexity arises when a project has many interacting parts, many people with different views, uncertainty about cause and effect, or ambiguity about what is even being asked. In complex situations, outcomes cannot be fully predicted from the start, however carefully you plan.

The principle asks you to recognise complexity when it is present and respond to it appropriately. Useful responses include breaking the work into smaller pieces, delivering in increments so you learn sooner, running experiments to test assumptions, building in options and slack, involving diverse perspectives, and keeping plans flexible where the uncertainty is greatest. The Cynefin framework, developed by Dave Snowden, is one widely used way of thinking about this: it distinguishes clear situations, where best practice applies, from complicated ones, which need expert analysis, complex ones, which need experiments, and chaotic ones, which need immediate action to restore stability.

## 10. Optimise risk responses
Risks are uncertain events or conditions that would affect the project's objectives if they occurred. Some are threats; some are opportunities. The principle asks you to identify and assess risks continually and to respond to them in proportion: neither ignoring them nor spending more on a response than the risk warrants.

Two words in this principle deserve attention. "Continually" means risk management is not a workshop at the start of the project but a habit throughout it. "Optimise" means responses should be appropriate to the significance of the risk, timely, realistic, agreed with the people who must carry them out, and owned by someone. Responding to opportunities, by making them more likely or more valuable, is as much a part of this as defending against threats. Chapter 14 covers risk in depth.

## 11. Embrace adaptability and resiliency
Things will not go as planned. Adaptability is the ability to respond to changing conditions; resiliency is the ability to absorb impacts and recover quickly from setbacks. The principle asks you to build both into the project.

In practice this means short feedback loops so that you notice change quickly, plans that focus on outcomes rather than fixing every detail, teams with overlapping skills so that one absence does not stop the work, reserves of time and money for things that go wrong, and a culture that treats setbacks as information rather than as occasions for blame. It also means being open to the possibility that the plan itself was wrong.

## 12. Enable change to achieve the envisioned future state
Projects change organisations, and organisations are made of people who may not want to change. The principle asks you to treat the human side of change as part of the project, not something that happens afterwards: preparing people for what is coming, helping them through the transition, and making sure the change is sustained.

The reasoning goes back to value. A new system, process or structure creates benefits only when people use it as intended. Change models such as John Kotter's eight steps, the ADKAR model, and William Bridges's work on transitions all make the same underlying point: people need to understand why the change is happening, want to take part, know how to work in the new way, and be supported until it becomes normal. Chapter 23 explores this in detail.

## When principles conflict
The principles are not a checklist to be satisfied one by one. They interact, and sometimes they pull in opposite directions.

- **Adaptability and stewardship.** Responding quickly to change can mean spending money on work that is later discarded. A steward must judge when adapting is worth its cost.
- **Stakeholder engagement and value.** Satisfying every stakeholder's request can dilute the result. Focusing on value sometimes means saying no to someone who matters.
- **Tailoring and quality.** Lightening the process saves effort, but the step you remove may be the one that would have caught a defect.
- **Collaboration and leadership.** Building consensus takes time; sometimes a leader has to decide before everyone agrees.

There is no formula for resolving these tensions. What the principles offer is a vocabulary for recognising them and discussing them openly, which is far better than resolving them by accident.

> example: Midway through Northfield's booking project, a group of senior consultants asked for online booking to be extended to a complex type of specialist referral that involved several departments. The request was reasonable and the consultants were influential (stakeholders). But it would delay the first release by four months, during which the morning phone queues would continue (value). Amira, the project manager, did not refuse or accept outright. She set out the trade-off for the sponsor, proposed delivering the simpler appointment types first and treating the referral pathway as a second increment (adaptability), and arranged for two consultants to help design it (collaboration). The sponsor agreed, and the consultants, having been involved, became advocates for the system.

## Principles in practice
How do you actually use principles day to day? A few habits help.

When you face a decision that no procedure covers, ask which principles are in play and what each suggests. If they agree, the decision is usually clear. If they conflict, you have found the real trade-off, and you can discuss it with the people who should decide.

When a process feels pointless, ask which principle it serves. If you can find one, the process may need explaining rather than abandoning. If you cannot, it may be ceremony, and tailoring it out may be right.

When you review a project, ask how well it honoured each principle. Lessons framed this way, such as "we engaged stakeholders late" or "we did not build quality into the integration work", transfer to the next project far better than lessons about specific documents or tools.

> myth: "Principles are too vague to be useful; what teams need is a method." Teams do need methods, but every method eventually meets a situation it did not anticipate. Principles are what let a team handle that situation well, and what let it adapt the method intelligently rather than either following it blindly or abandoning it.

> exam: The PMP exam's situational questions are, in effect, tests of these principles. When two answers both sound plausible, ask which better reflects stewardship, collaboration, stakeholder engagement, a focus on value, and building quality in. The answer that involves the team, engages affected stakeholders, protects value and deals with the root cause usually wins over one that is quick, unilateral or cosmetic.

## Key ideas
- Processes solve foreseen problems; principles guide judgement when the unforeseen happens.
- The twelve principles cover stewardship, collaboration, stakeholders, value, systems thinking, leadership, tailoring, quality, complexity, risk, adaptability and resilience, and enabling change.
- Each principle has a reason behind it, usually learned from experience of projects that ignored it.
- Principles can conflict. Naming the conflict openly is the first step to resolving it well.
- Use principles to make decisions, to evaluate processes, and to draw lessons that transfer.

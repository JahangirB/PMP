why: Projects concentrate conflict: scarce resources, competing priorities, tight deadlines, different professional values and people who must work closely without having chosen each other. Conflict cannot be avoided, and should not be, because some of it is how good ideas are tested. What matters is handling it well. The same goes for negotiation and decision-making, which together decide most of what a project actually does.

## Where conflict comes from
Studies of project conflict have long found that its most common sources are not personalities, though personality clashes get the most attention. Classic research by Hans Thamhain and David Wilemon ranked the main sources as schedules, priorities, resources, technical opinions, administrative procedures, cost, and only then personalities. Most project conflict is about the work.

That finding is useful, because conflicts about the work can usually be resolved by addressing the work: clarifying priorities, making trade-offs explicit, involving the right decision-maker. Many apparent personality clashes turn out, on closer inspection, to be disagreements about priorities or resources that have become personal because nobody resolved them.

Other common sources include unclear roles, ambiguous goals, poor communication, differences in values or culture, competition for scarce resources, and incompatible incentives, such as a functional manager rewarded for departmental efficiency while the project needs her people's time.

## Healthy and unhealthy conflict
Not all conflict is bad. **Task conflict**, disagreement about ideas, approaches and decisions, can improve outcomes by exposing weaknesses and generating better options, as long as people trust each other and the disagreement stays focused on the problem. **Relationship conflict**, personal friction and animosity, almost always harms performance, consuming energy and undermining trust.

The project manager's aim is therefore not to eliminate conflict but to keep it productive: to encourage open debate about the work while preventing it from becoming personal. Teams with psychological safety (Chapter 20) are better at this, because people can disagree without feeling attacked.

## Ways of handling conflict
Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann described five approaches to conflict, based on two dimensions: how far a person tries to satisfy their own concerns (assertiveness) and how far they try to satisfy the other's (cooperativeness). Project management literature often uses closely related labels.

- **Collaborating**, or **problem solving**: working together to find a solution that fully satisfies both sides. High assertiveness, high cooperation. It takes time and trust, but produces the best and most lasting resolutions, and addresses the underlying cause.
- **Compromising**, or **reconciling**: each side gives up something to reach an acceptable middle. It is quicker, but often leaves both sides partly dissatisfied and the underlying issue unresolved.
- **Accommodating**, or **smoothing**: emphasising areas of agreement and conceding to the other side. It preserves relationships and suits issues that matter more to the other person, but repeated use builds resentment and leaves real problems unsolved.
- **Competing**, or **forcing**: pursuing one's own position at the other's expense, often using authority. It is fast and sometimes necessary in emergencies or for unpopular but essential decisions, but it damages relationships if used often.
- **Avoiding**, or **withdrawing**: postponing or sidestepping the conflict. It can be useful to let tempers cool or when the issue is trivial, but problems avoided usually grow.

No approach is always right. Collaborating is generally preferred for important issues where there is time and goodwill, because it resolves the cause rather than the symptom. But a skilled project manager chooses deliberately: forcing a safety decision, accommodating on a matter that is trivial to the project but important to someone else, withdrawing briefly from a heated meeting to resume when calmer.

A few practical habits help with most conflicts: address conflict early, before positions harden; deal with it privately rather than in public; focus on the issue and its effects, not on personalities; listen to understand each side's underlying interests; and make sure the resolution is clear and followed through.

## Negotiation
**Negotiation** is a discussion aimed at reaching an agreement between parties with different interests. Project managers negotiate constantly: for people with functional managers, for scope and dates with sponsors, for prices and terms with suppliers, for priorities with other projects.

The most influential modern approach comes from Roger Fisher and William Ury's book *Getting to Yes*, based on work at the Harvard Negotiation Project. It proposes **principled negotiation**, built on four ideas.

- **Separate the people from the problem.** Treat the other party as a partner in solving a shared problem, while being firm on the substance.
- **Focus on interests, not positions.** A position is what someone says they want ("we need the system live by March"); an interest is why they want it ("the old contract expires in April and renewal is expensive"). Interests often allow solutions that positions do not.
- **Invent options for mutual gain** before deciding, rather than fighting over a single option.
- **Insist on objective criteria**: market prices, standards, precedent, expert opinion, so that the outcome rests on principle rather than pressure.

They also introduced the idea of a **BATNA**, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you will do if no agreement is reached. Knowing your BATNA tells you when to walk away; improving it strengthens your position; and estimating the other side's BATNA tells you how much room there is. A negotiator without a clear BATNA may accept a bad deal for fear of no deal.

Negotiation outcomes are often described as **win-win**, where both sides gain; **win-lose**, where one gains at the other's expense; or **lose-lose**, where both are worse off. On projects, where the same people must keep working together, win-lose outcomes are usually costly, because the losing side has many chances to make the winner's life difficult later.

> example: Northfield needed one of the trust's two database specialists for six weeks, and the head of IT, who managed him, refused: her team was already behind on a security upgrade. Rather than escalate, Amira asked what the upgrade needed. It turned out the specialist's most time-consuming task was migrating test data, which the booking project's supplier could do quickly with its own tools. Amira offered the supplier's help with the migration in exchange for the specialist's time. The upgrade finished early, and the booking project got its specialist.

## Making decisions
Projects run on decisions, and the quality of those decisions shapes the outcome more than almost anything else. Two questions matter for any decision: who should make it, and how?

Who decides should be clear before the decision arises. Some decisions belong to the project manager, some to the team, some to the sponsor or a governance body, and some to the product owner. Unclear decision rights produce either paralysis, where nobody decides, or conflict, where several people decide differently. A RACI chart or similar responsibility matrix, which shows who is responsible, accountable, consulted and informed for each decision or deliverable, helps make this explicit.

How to decide depends on the decision. Common approaches include:

- **Autocratic**: one person decides. Fast, and appropriate when time is short, the decision is within one person's expertise, or the team expects it.
- **Consultative**: one person decides after hearing others' views. A good balance for many project decisions.
- **Majority vote**: quick for a group, but can leave a large minority uncommitted.
- **Consensus**: the group discusses until everyone can accept the decision. Slower, but produces strong commitment.
- **Unanimity**: everyone actively agrees. Rarely practical for significant decisions.

Adaptive teams use several lightweight group techniques. **Fist of five** asks each person to show from zero to five fingers to indicate their support, revealing reservations quickly. **Roman voting** uses thumbs up, sideways or down. **Dot voting** gives each person a few votes to distribute across options, useful for prioritising. And **planning poker**, used for estimation, asks everyone to reveal their estimate at once, so that people are not anchored by the first number spoken.

## Better decisions under uncertainty
Research on judgement, especially by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, has shown how predictably human decisions go wrong. A few biases are especially common on projects.

- **Anchoring**: the first number mentioned, such as an executive's hoped-for date, pulls every later estimate towards it.
- **Confirmation bias**: people seek and remember evidence that supports what they already believe.
- **Sunk cost fallacy**: continuing because of what has already been spent (Chapter 4).
- **Groupthink**: a cohesive group suppresses dissent to preserve harmony, and reaches poor decisions with confidence.
- **Overconfidence**: experts in particular are often more certain than their track record justifies.

Practical defences include gathering independent views before discussion (as planning poker does), assigning someone to argue the opposite case, using the outside view (Chapter 9), running a pre-mortem (Chapter 14), separating the decision from the people who proposed the options, and recording the reasons for decisions so that they can be learned from later.

> myth: "Good teams don't have conflict." Good teams have plenty of conflict about ideas and very little about each other. A team that never disagrees is usually either not engaging with the hard questions or not feeling safe enough to say what it thinks.

> exam: Expect questions on the conflict resolution techniques and which is best in a given situation (collaborate or problem-solve is usually preferred; force only for emergencies or essential decisions; withdraw only temporarily or for trivial issues), on the common sources of project conflict, and on negotiation principles such as interests over positions and win-win outcomes. Scenario questions often ask what the project manager should do first: usually, meet the people involved privately and understand the issue before acting. Know group decision techniques such as fist of five and planning poker.

## Key ideas
- Most project conflict is about schedules, priorities, resources and technical choices, not personalities.
- Task conflict can improve decisions; relationship conflict harms teams. Keep disagreement about the work.
- Collaborating solves the underlying problem and is usually preferred; compromising, accommodating, forcing and avoiding each have their place.
- Principled negotiation separates people from the problem, focuses on interests, invents options and uses objective criteria; know your best alternative to agreement.
- Decide who decides before decisions arise; choose a decision method that fits the decision.
- Predictable biases distort project decisions; independent views, the outside view and pre-mortems help.

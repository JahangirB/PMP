title: The project manager's job
summary: What a project manager actually does, the competencies the role needs, and how to lead when you hold little formal authority.
why: Job titles vary wildly, and many people who manage projects do not have "project manager" in theirs. Underneath the variety, the role has a stable core. Understanding that core helps you see where your effort makes the most difference, and why so much of the job is about people rather than plans.

## A role, not a rank
A project manager is the person assigned by the organisation to lead the team responsible for achieving the project's objectives. That is the whole formal definition, and it says nothing about seniority, technical background or department. Project managers may be senior executives running national infrastructure, or a team member in a small firm who also does half the technical work. Some organisations have no one with the title at all; the role is filled by a team lead, a product manager or a business analyst.

What makes someone a project manager is accountability for the whole. Specialists on a project are responsible for their part: the design, the code, the training material, the contract. The project manager is responsible for the parts coming together into something that achieves its purpose, on terms the organisation can accept. That accountability for integration is the heart of the role.

## The integrator
Every project is a set of pieces that must fit: scope, schedule, budget, quality, risk, people, suppliers, stakeholders. A change in one ripples through the others. Adding a feature affects the schedule, the budget, the test plan, the training and perhaps a contract. Losing a key engineer affects the schedule, the risk profile and the morale of the rest of the team.

Specialists, quite properly, optimise their own piece. The designer wants the best design, the finance team wants the lowest cost, the sales team wants the earliest date. Only the project manager is paid to look across all of them and make the trade-offs that serve the project as a whole. This is why integration is often described as the knowledge area that ties the others together, and why change control (Chapter 16) is so central: it is the mechanism through which those trade-offs are made deliberately rather than by accident.

Integration also happens at the level of thinking. The project manager has to hold together the strategic view (why are we doing this, and is it still worth it?), the operational view (what is happening this week, and what is stuck?) and the human view (how is the team, and who is worried about what?). Experienced project managers move between these levels constantly.

## What project managers spend their time on
Ask project managers what fills their days and the answers are consistent: talking to people. Practitioners often quote a figure that project managers spend around ninety per cent of their time communicating. The exact number is folklore rather than research, but the point is sound. Meetings, conversations, messages, reports, negotiations, presentations and the quiet word in the corridor are where most of the work happens.

The rest of the time goes on activities that support those conversations: planning and replanning, tracking progress, analysing problems, preparing decisions, managing risks, checking quality, handling contracts and, in smaller projects, doing some of the technical work. Paperwork matters, but it exists to support decisions and shared understanding, not for its own sake. A plan that nobody reads, or a risk register that nobody acts on, is a cost without a benefit.

It helps to think of a few recurring roles the project manager plays.

- **Planner.** Turning the purpose into an approach and a plan the team believes in, and keeping it current.
- **Coordinator.** Making sure the right people are working on the right things in the right order, and that hand-offs between them happen.
- **Communicator.** Keeping everyone with a stake informed, in the form and at the frequency that suits them.
- **Problem solver.** Removing obstacles, resolving conflicts and making or escalating decisions so the work can continue.
- **Steward.** Spending the organisation's money and people's time responsibly, and being honest about how things stand.
- **Leader.** Giving the team a sense of purpose and direction, and creating the conditions in which they can do their best work.

## The competencies the role needs
PMI describes the competencies a project professional needs in three groups, which it calls the talent triangle. The current labels are ways of working, power skills and business acumen.

**Ways of working** are the technical skills of project management: planning, scheduling, estimating, budgeting, risk analysis, quality techniques and the practices of predictive, adaptive and hybrid approaches. They are what most training courses teach, and they are necessary. Without them a project manager cannot build a credible plan or tell whether a project is on track.

**Power skills** are the interpersonal ones: leadership, communication, negotiation, collaboration, emotional intelligence, problem solving, a growth mindset. They used to be called soft skills, a name that undersold them. In practice they are usually what separates a project manager who keeps things moving from one who merely documents why they stopped.

**Business acumen** is understanding the organisation and its environment: its strategy, how it makes or saves money, its customers and competitors, the regulations it works under, and how the project contributes. A project manager with business acumen can talk to executives in their terms, notice when the business case has quietly stopped being true, and make trade-offs that favour the outcomes the organisation actually needs.

The three are not separate tracks. A good risk discussion with a sponsor uses all three at once: technical skill to analyse the risk, business acumen to judge what it means for the organisation, and power skills to present it so that a decision gets made.

## Authority and influence
One of the realities of the role is that project managers usually have more responsibility than authority. They are accountable for results but often do not manage the people doing the work. Team members may report to functional managers who decide their priorities, their pay and their careers. Suppliers report to their own companies. Stakeholders who can make or break the project report to nobody the project manager can call on.

How much formal authority a project manager has depends largely on the organisation's structure, which Chapter 4 describes. In a strongly project-oriented organisation, the project manager may control the budget and direct the team full-time. In a functional organisation, they may be a part-time coordinator with almost no authority at all. Most organisations sit somewhere between, in some form of matrix where team members answer to both a functional manager and a project manager.

Because formal authority is limited, project managers rely on other sources of influence. Researchers John French and Bertram Raven described several bases of power in the 1950s, and their categories are still widely used: legitimate power from position, reward and coercive power from the ability to give or withhold things people value, expert power from knowledge, and referent power from being respected and liked. Informational power, from controlling information others need, was added later. Project managers tend to have little of the first three and must build the others. Being competent, well informed, fair, reliable and genuinely helpful turns out to be a more durable basis for influence than position.

> example: In the running case, Northfield's project manager, Amira, has no line authority over the clinic managers whose staff must change how they book appointments. She builds influence by spending her first month visiting clinics, listening to how booking actually works, and fixing two small irritations in the old system along the way. When she later needs the clinic managers to release staff for training, they agree, because she has shown she understands their work and can be trusted to keep their interests in view.

## Leadership and management
Writers have long distinguished management, which is about plans, processes, control and order, from leadership, which is about direction, alignment, motivation and change. The distinction is useful as long as nobody takes it to mean that a project manager can choose one. Projects need both. Without management, a project has energy but no coherence; without leadership, it has plans but no commitment.

The style of leadership the role calls for has shifted. Command-and-control leadership, in which the leader decides and the team carries out, fits work that is routine, urgent and well understood. Much project work is none of those. It depends on knowledge workers who often know more about their part than the project manager does, and on their willingness to solve problems the plan did not foresee. This is why **servant leadership**, an idea associated with Robert Greenleaf, has become central to modern practice, especially in adaptive teams. A servant leader sees the job as enabling the team: removing obstacles, protecting it from distraction, securing what it needs, helping it grow, and trusting it to decide how to do its work.

Servant leadership is sometimes misunderstood as passivity. It is not. A servant leader still holds a clear view of the goal, still has difficult conversations, and still makes decisions when the team cannot. The difference is where the leader's attention goes: to the team's capacity to deliver, rather than to the leader's own control over the work. Part IV discusses leadership in more depth.

## Adaptive teams and the project manager
In teams using Scrum, the formal roles are a product owner, who decides what is most valuable to build next, a Scrum Master, who coaches the team and the organisation in the method and removes impediments, and the developers. There is no project manager in the framework. This sometimes leads people to conclude that adaptive work does not need project management.

What actually happens is that the responsibilities of project management are distributed. The product owner takes on much of the work of scope and value. The Scrum Master takes on much of the work of process and team health. The team plans and tracks its own work. Outside the team, someone still has to coordinate several teams working on the same product, manage dependencies on other parts of the organisation, handle budgets and contracts, report to governance, manage risks that cross team boundaries, and engage stakeholders the team never meets. In larger efforts, those responsibilities are usually held by someone with a project, programme or delivery manager title. The work of project management does not disappear; its shape changes.

## The project manager's sphere of influence
It is useful to picture the project manager working at four levels.

- **Within the project**, leading the team, managing the plan and integrating the work.
- **Within the organisation**, working with the sponsor, steering groups, functional managers, the project management office and other project managers competing for the same people and money.
- **Within the industry**, keeping up with technology, standards, regulation and practices that affect the work.
- **Within the profession**, learning continuously, sharing knowledge, mentoring others and acting ethically.

The further out you go, the less formal authority the project manager has and the more their effectiveness depends on reputation, relationships and judgement. Good project managers invest at every level, not just the first.

## What the job is not
A few misconceptions about the role cause real harm.

- **Not the person who does all the work.** In small projects the project manager may also be a technical contributor, but a project manager who disappears into the technical work stops integrating, and the project drifts.
- **Not an administrator.** Producing reports and minutes is part of the job; being reduced to it is a sign that real decisions are being made elsewhere.
- **Not the owner of the business case.** The sponsor owns the reason for the project and the benefits. The project manager delivers the means and must say clearly when the reason is in danger.
- **Not the hero.** Projects that depend on one person working nights to rescue them are fragile. A good project manager builds a team and a system that do not need heroics.

> myth: "A good project manager should know the technology better than the team." It helps to understand the work well enough to ask good questions and spot nonsense, but the team's specialists will know more about their parts. The project manager's distinctive expertise is integration, people and judgement.

> exam: The PMP exam is written from the perspective of a project manager who is proactive, collaborative and accountable. In situational questions, the right answer usually has the project manager taking ownership of a problem, investigating before acting, working with the team and stakeholders rather than around them, and escalating only when the issue is genuinely beyond their authority. Answers that blame others, wait passively, or act unilaterally without consulting the people affected are usually wrong.

## Key ideas
- A project manager is the person accountable for the team achieving the project's objectives. The defining responsibility is integration: making the pieces fit and making the trade-offs.
- Most of the job is communication; documents exist to support decisions and shared understanding.
- The competencies are ways of working, power skills and business acumen, and good practice uses all three together.
- Project managers usually have more responsibility than authority, so they build influence through expertise, information, fairness and trust.
- Projects need both management and leadership. Servant leadership puts the leader's attention on the team's ability to deliver.
- In adaptive teams the responsibilities of project management are shared among roles; they do not vanish.

title: Thinking like a project manager
summary: The habits of thought that experienced project managers share: seeing systems, finding causes, acting early, checking assumptions, weighing trade-offs and staying honest about uncertainty.
why: Methods, tools and templates can be learned from books. What distinguishes experienced project managers is how they think when the book runs out: how they read a situation, which questions they ask first, what they notice that others miss, and how they decide under pressure. This chapter draws together the mindset that runs through the whole of this book.

## Start from purpose
The first habit is to keep asking what the project is for. Every plan, document, meeting and decision is a means to an end, and it is remarkably easy to lose sight of the end. When a team argues about a feature, a good project manager asks which option better serves the outcome the organisation needs. When a process feels burdensome, they ask what purpose it serves. When the plan and reality diverge, they ask whether the original goal is still the right one.

Purpose gives decisions a test. Without it, decisions are settled by whoever is loudest, most senior or most persistent. With it, they can be settled by argument about what actually helps.

## See the system
Projects are systems of interacting parts (Chapter 5), and many project problems are caused not by any single part but by how the parts interact. A team's slow delivery may be caused by approval delays in another department; a quality problem by an incentive that rewards speed; a supplier's poor performance by a contract that punishes them for raising issues.

Systems thinking asks a few habitual questions. What else will this change affect? What happens next, and after that? Who else is involved that we have not considered? Are we treating a symptom or a cause? Where are the delays, feedback loops and incentives in this situation?

It also guards against a common trap: the obvious fix that makes things worse. Adding people to a late project, adding inspection to fix quality, adding reports to fix visibility and adding pressure to fix speed all seem sensible and often backfire, because they ignore how the system responds.

## Find the cause
When something goes wrong, the instinct is to fix it quickly. Experienced project managers fix the immediate problem and then ask why it happened. A defect is fixed; then the question is how it got through. A missed deadline is recovered; then the question is why the estimate was wrong. A stakeholder is upset; then the question is what we failed to understand about them.

Techniques such as the five whys and cause-and-effect diagrams (Chapter 13) help, but the habit matters more than the technique: a refusal to accept the first explanation, especially if it blames an individual. Most problems on projects have causes in the system, the plan or the process, and fixing those prevents the next problem; blaming a person rarely does.

## Act early
Problems on projects are almost always cheaper to address early than late: risks before they occur, conflicts before positions harden, scope disagreements before work is built, bad news before it surprises the sponsor. Being **proactive** means looking ahead for trouble and dealing with it while it is still small.

Acting early has a counterpart: not acting rashly. The most consistent advice in situational judgement, including on the exam, is to **assess before acting**. When something happens, the first step is usually to understand it: gather facts, talk to the people involved, analyse the impact. Then decide. A project manager who reacts instantly to every report, reassigning people, escalating or changing the plan before understanding the situation, creates as much disruption as one who never acts at all.

## Check assumptions
Every plan rests on assumptions, and most project surprises are assumptions that turned out to be false. Experienced project managers make assumptions explicit, ask which ones the plan depends on most, and look for cheap ways to test them early. "We assume users will book online" can be tested with a pilot clinic long before forty clinics are committed.

The same habit applies to beliefs about people. "The vendor isn't interested in fixing this" or "finance will never approve that" may be true, or may be a story nobody has checked. A direct conversation often reveals a different reality.

## Think in ranges and probabilities
Projects are uncertain, and good project managers think and speak accordingly. Instead of "we will finish in March", they say "most likely late March, with a reasonable chance of April; here is what would push it later". Instead of "this risk won't happen", they say "it's unlikely, but if it does, here's what we'll do".

This is not hedging. It is honesty about what is known, and it helps decision-makers. A sponsor told that March is certain will make commitments that fail. A sponsor told the range can plan for it. Thinking in probabilities also helps the project manager notice when evidence should change their view: when the first three integrations each took twice as long as estimated, the fourth probably will too.

## Make trade-offs explicit
Almost every significant project decision is a trade-off: scope against time, speed against quality, cost against risk, one stakeholder's needs against another's. Inexperienced project managers often try to avoid trade-offs, promising everything, or make them implicitly, letting quality slip silently to protect a date. Experienced ones make them visible and take them to the people who should decide.

Making a trade-off explicit usually means setting out the options and their consequences in terms the decision-maker cares about: "we can keep the date by deferring these two features, or keep the features by moving the date three weeks, or keep both by adding two developers at this cost and this risk". Once the trade-off is visible, decisions get made, and the project manager is no longer quietly absorbing pressure that belongs to someone else.

## Work through people
Projects are delivered by people, and much of a project manager's thinking is about them: who needs to be involved, who is worried and why, who has the knowledge, who needs to decide, who will be affected. The habit is to treat people's concerns as information rather than obstacles, to involve the people affected by decisions, and to build the relationships that make future problems easier to solve.

It also means **servant leadership** in everyday form: asking the team what is getting in their way and removing it, giving them the context to make good decisions, protecting them from distraction, and giving credit generously.

## Know your role and its limits
Experienced project managers are clear about what is theirs to decide and what is not. They take full ownership within their authority, and they do not exceed it: decisions that belong to the sponsor, the product owner, a governance board or another manager are taken to them, with analysis and a recommendation. They escalate when it is genuinely necessary, not as a way of avoiding difficult conversations, and not too late.

The same clarity applies to accountability. When things go wrong, they own their part openly. When things go well, they credit the team.

## Adapt the approach
No method is right for every project or every stage of one. The experienced project manager treats methods as tools, chooses among them deliberately (Chapter 8), and changes course when the situation changes. They are equally comfortable writing a detailed plan for a predictable piece of work and running short experiments where nothing can be predicted. What they will not do is follow a method mechanically when it is clearly not working, or abandon one without understanding what it was protecting against.

## Keep learning
Finally, the experienced project manager treats every project as a source of learning: reflecting on what happened and why, seeking feedback, reading, talking to other practitioners, and changing how they work as a result. The field itself keeps changing, as the history in Chapter 2 shows, and the best practitioners change with it.

## The mindset on the exam
The PMP exam's situational questions are, in effect, tests of these habits. A few patterns recur, and they follow directly from this chapter.

- **Assess first.** When something happens, understand it before acting: review, analyse, talk to the people involved.
- **Fix causes, not symptoms.** Prefer answers that address why a problem happened.
- **Work with people.** Prefer collaboration, private conversation and involvement over unilateral action, public criticism or bypassing people.
- **Use the right process and the right decision-maker.** Changes go through change control; decisions beyond your authority go to whoever holds it.
- **Be proactive and honest.** Raise risks and bad news early, with options.
- **Serve the team.** Remove obstacles; let the team decide how to do its work.
- **Keep value in view.** Prefer answers that protect the outcome the project exists for.

These patterns are not tricks for passing an exam. They describe how good project managers actually work.

> example: Looking back on Northfield's project, Amira wrote that the decisions she was proudest of were rarely technical. Asking the sponsor what success meant at the start; visiting clinics before designing anything; reporting the cost overrun early; slowing the rollout during the restructure; replacing classroom training with clinic champions. Each one came from asking what the project was really for, who it affected, and what was actually happening, before acting.

> myth: "Experience is the number of projects you have run." Experience is what you learned from them. Someone who has run ten projects without reflecting may have one project's experience ten times. Deliberate reflection is what turns time into judgement.

> exam: Every chapter's exam box points to this one. When two answers look plausible, choose the one that understands the situation first, involves the right people, addresses the cause, follows the proper process, and serves the project's purpose.

## Key ideas
- Start from purpose; it gives every decision a test.
- See the system: consider interactions, delays and incentives; beware fixes that backfire.
- Find root causes in the system rather than blaming individuals.
- Act early, but assess before acting.
- Make assumptions explicit and test them; think and speak in ranges.
- Make trade-offs visible and take them to the right decision-maker.
- Work through people, know the limits of your role, adapt the approach, and keep learning.
